# Interlude — Selling Ads, Buying Ads, and Official Mirrors

This interlude is a bench, not a second book.

Book 3’s main argument is about **buying** attention for a product that already fulfills a promise. The Sea handbook’s Ads volume also spends many pages on **selling** attention, through a site that wants to host ads or to earn a commission. Those are different first principles. Mixing their metrics is how a product team starts talking about RPM as if it were a sign of fit, or a publisher starts buying traffic and calling the click-through rate a market.

If you are in a hurry and you only sell Thursday Packet, steal the perimeter table, skip the publisher path, and go back to Chapter 7. If you also run a content site, or you might, stay for the inspection.

---

## Two Jobs, Two Scoreboards

| | Selling ads on a site (AdSense-like, plus later commissions) | Buying ads for a product (search or social spend) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What you are doing | Convincing advertisers the site is worth paying for | Paying to put a real person in front of your promise sooner |
| What “good” looks like | Trust, useful pages, experience, a rate per thousand that is not a vanity of raw visits | A defined conversion, intent phrases, complete tracking, a learning period you can survive |
| Common misread | “If we add words and sites, we will pass review” | “If we get clicks, we found a market” |

**Risks**

1. Using a publisher rate card to judge a product campaign.  
2. Using a product click-through rate to judge a media site.  
3. Delaying both jobs for months because the two paths were treated as one identity crisis.

**Suggestion.** Pick the job you are doing *this quarter* and keep the other scoreboard in a drawer. A content site can later add commissions. A product can later run a modest brand-term campaign. The mix is allowed. The blender is not.

**Conclusion.** First principles first. The shared word “ads” is a pun, not a strategy.

## If You Are Building a Media Site (Perimeter Only)

The handbook’s publisher path is a quality argument in disguise. A review is not a formality. It is, in that telling, a stand-in for an advertiser asking whether the site is worth money. A refusal that only says the content failed a quality standard is a prompt to inspect **low-value pages**, not a prompt to add adjectives.

I will not reprint a twelve-month income story as a plan. The handbook includes path sketches — build, then traffic, then apply, then tune — and case shapes (a UX pass that moved a monthly figure, a localized topic that moved another, an AI-tools review site that later sat in a mid-thousands-per-month range). Those are shapes. They are not Ignea, AdaptLane, or Thursday Packet forecasts. They are certainly not a reason to plant thirty thin sites and hope a tenth of them pass.

A short self-check, if this is actually your job:

- Each page should carry at least one fact or judgment a rewrite mill would not have. Length is not quality.  
- Commercial links should be sparse enough that a reader still meets a page, not a hallway of exits.  
- Experience, authorship, and an About / Privacy / Contact set are not decorations.  
- Speed, mobile use, and the absence of hostile pop-ups are part of the product.  
- Some real search visits before you apply is a practical caution in the teaching archive, not an official public threshold you should invent and then tattoo.  
- Never click your own ads. The handbook’s warning is the only intensity I will copy: the ban risk is not a folklore to test.

RPM ranges in the archive (finance and law at the high end, hobby and niche-collecting at the low end; English often stronger than Chinese in the same telling) are for **order-of-magnitude thinking** when you choose a topic, not for a spreadsheet called “Year 1.” A site can have traffic and still be a poor advertising surface. A teaching example in the archive is a narrow collectible topic with a low rate. Demand and monetization are not the same fire test.

After approval, the usual levers are topic choice, placement experiments (a teaching habit is to let automatic placements run a while before carving them by heat), mobile checks, internal links that deepen a visit, and caution with interstitial formats whose rules move. Policy pages go stale. The official document wins.

Commissions can sit on top of a site that already has traffic. Entry points for large official affiliate programs change, and old tutorials are noise. Confirm the current door before you staff a content plan on a memory of 2016.

Payout and foreign-exchange chores — a bank wire, a legal-name spelling, a SWIFT code, an annual conversion quota — are operations, not growth hacks. Fill them in once, correctly, against the official form and your bank’s current rules. I will not turn a China Merchants Bank teaching walkthrough into your finance advice. If that is your bank, read the bank.

**Conclusion, for this perimeter:** become a site an advertiser would pay to stand on, then apply. Do not use this interlude as a product-market-fit story.

## Mirrors You Can Use Before You Spend (Product Side)

Three official or official-adjacent mirrors show up in the handbook as ways to see **paid behavior**, not as oracles.

1. **The official ads transparency center.** You can see ads a domain has run, and sometimes where else an advertiser has been willing to spend. That is useful before you buy a phrase, before you decide a topic is only a rumor, and before you chase a partnership. You will not see their keywords or their bids. Someone else spending is not a promise you will earn. It is evidence that money has already been willing to stand there.  
2. **Title research and monitors** (third-party tools in this class come and go). Headlines are often the difference between a click and a shrug. A title that has been paid for, repeatedly, is a structure to rewrite — not a sentence to steal.  
3. **Live search results on your actual phrase.** See which ads keep showing up. Cross with the transparency center and with any “site-to-phrase / phrase-to-site” map you trust enough to treat as an estimate.

A health-check skill or export review can sit beside these mirrors as a diagnostic. It still is not a license to autopilot the account.

**Boundary.** Mirrors reduce some tuition. They do not replace a completable promise, a green tracking sheet, or a landing sentence a tired person can understand.

## A Ninety-Minute Bench (When You Are About to Blend the Two Jobs)

1. Write one sentence: “This quarter I am selling ads on a site” or “This quarter I am buying attention for a product.” If you wrote both, pick a primary anyway.  
2. If buying: open the transparency center for two comparables, write five intent phrases, and refuse to open the coupon tab until Chapter 7’s sheet is green.  
3. If selling: run the low-value self-check on three pages, and write down one unique fact each page actually contains. If you cannot, you do not have an application problem. You have a page problem.  
4. Put the other scoreboard in a drawer. Tell a teammate which drawer.

## Where This Interlude Falsifies Itself

It fails if “different first principles” becomes a purity test that forbids a product from later publishing useful pages, or a publisher from ever buying a brand term.

It fails if RPM tables are copied into a pitch deck.

It fails if transparency-center tourism becomes a new way to avoid shipping the hook and the tracking sheet.

It fails if payout instructions are followed from this book years later, when forms have changed.

---

## Takeaway

**Rule:** Selling ads and buying ads share a word, not a scoreboard. Official mirrors show paid behavior; they do not confer a market.

**This week:** Name which job you are doing. Keep the other metric out of the meeting.

**Not a promise:** AdSense path sketches, RPM ranges, monthly income case shapes, and competitor-spend sightings are teaching materials, not a forecast.
