# Chapter 6 — Ads Buy Time, Not a Verdict

The Thursday Packet coupon tab had been open for eleven days, which is a long time to hover over a gift and still refuse to read the instructions.

The builder’s fear was not really the invoice. The invoice, at the modest ceiling they could have chosen, was smaller than a week of cathedral design. The fear was being forced to look at a number that was not a click-through rate: a purchase, or the absence of one, attached to money they could no longer pretend was “just testing creatives.”

Paid acquisition is where this book’s title stops being a slogan. You can buy a shorter wait for a signal. You cannot buy a ceremony that declares fit. Used as a decision instrument, ads ask whether someone will click, register, use, pay, and return — from which phrases, from which countries, and at which step the journey dies. Used as a mood, ads become a way to purchase the feeling that the street has finally noticed you.

This chapter is the instrument: a coupon you claim before the first click, a conservative start, official hygiene, and two handbook shapes that must not become your business plan. Tracking, keywords, and the learning period get their own chapter next, because that is where good starts go to be ruined.

---

## Not “Burn Money for Users”

**Risks**

1. Treating spend as a character test — either “real companies advertise” or “ads are a casino” — instead of as a clock.  
2. Killing a channel after a few empty days, or blessing it after a pretty click-through rate.  
3. Watching impressions and clicks as if they were a market.

**Suggestion.** Write the questions the money is allowed to answer this fortnight, and refuse to spend on a question you cannot instrument:

- Will anyone who wants this job click, register, use, pay, or return?  
- From which phrases or rooms, and from which countries, do they come?  
- Which step of the journey dies?

A large number of durable products, in the handbook’s telling, passed through a paid stage. The reusable value was **cash exchanged for time**, and the honesty of negative signals — an early stop is a kind of gift. That is not the same claim as “ads always work,” or “ads will raise your revenue.”

**Conclusion.** Paid acquisition is a decision tool. It is not a personality, and it is not a substitute for a broom or a loop.

---

## Certainty First, Then Volume

A public list of official advertising recommendations, summarized in the handbook, points in one direction I will keep in plain language:

> Do not rush volume. First do the things you can actually make certain.

Certainty, here, is not a promise of profit. It is the boring stack: a clear goal, a budget that follows results instead of spreading itself like butter, tight geography, phrases and audiences that start precise, conversion tracking that is complete, a simple campaign structure, and automated bidding only after there is something real to learn from.

If that list sounds conservative, it is because the expensive mistakes are almost always impatience dressed as courage.

---

## Cold Start Is Restraint, Not a Heroic First Day

The handbook’s consensus for a first campaign, rewritten for this desk:

1. Survive, then discuss volume.  
2. Prefer countries that have already produced an order.  
3. Prefer phrases with clear intent, a moderate volume, and a cost-per-click you can stand — not trophy keywords that will eat a week by Tuesday.  
4. Early goals may be **clicks**, with a cap, until you have real purchases from more than one person; then you may move toward **conversions**.  
5. Raise budgets slowly. Emotional doubling is not a strategy.  
6. Results are not linear. A few empty days are the moment people throw away the learning they just paid for.

A teaching parameter in one recap: wait until about **five distinct purchasing users** before you gradually switch the campaign toward a purchase goal. A teaching ceiling in the same cluster: many early days were kept to about **100 HKD** or less. I will not make those numbers your religion. I will say why they exist: you need more than a single lucky card to teach a system, and you need a ceiling that makes an empty week survivable.

Thursday Packet, if it ever opened the coupon tab properly, would start where an order had already happened, on phrases close to garbled names and Thursday exports, with a click cap and a daily ceiling. It would not start on “AI design tools” because that phrase sounded like the future.

## Claim the Coupon Before You Teach the Account

**Risks**

1. Creating the first click, then discovering that a new-customer credit cannot be attached after spend has started.  
2. Treating the credit as profit instead of as a cheaper first lesson.  
3. Spending the credit on a trophy phrase, then calling ads “impossible.”

**Suggestion — order of operations, not folklore:**

1. While **logged out**, go through the official ads coupon entry, not a random blog’s expired map.  
2. Pick the rebate tier you actually intend to qualify for (the handbook mentions common “spend then rebate” rungs in the hundreds to low thousands of dollars — check the live page).  
3. Then sign in or create the account and bind it.  
4. Confirm a progress bar or the equivalent in the official interface before you congratulate yourself.

The handbook includes a genuine miss: spend began, and the credit could not be recovered on that account. The salvage, in that telling, was to treat the current account as a learning account and to do the sequence correctly on the next project with a fresh official entry. That is a scar, not a life hack.

**Conclusion.** Confirm the credit before the first click. If you already missed it, do not invent a back door. Write the lesson down and stop adding mythology to the miss.

## Official Twelve, Kept as a Desk List

I will not pretend these are my commandments. They are a compressed official hygiene list from the handbook’s Google Ads chapter, in the order you can tape above a monitor:

1. Choose the goal before you spend (conversion, install, sign-up, or exposure). A cold start may begin with clicks, then move when data exists.  
2. Let budgets follow results. Raise what works. Cut what does not. Avoid even-handed spending as a moral pose.  
3. Write the user’s job, not your feature pride.  
4. Tighten geography first; widen only with evidence.  
5. Cap frequency on display or video so you do not become furniture, or an enemy.  
6. Start precise (phrases, topics, placements), then widen.  
7. Put new audiences or placements in **observation** before you bet on them.  
8. Keep campaign structure simple; merge early if you can.  
9. Match the ad type to the job (search to catch demand, display to be seen, video to tell a story).  
10. Use automated bidding after conversion data exists — not as a wish.  
11. Keep conversion tracking complete, and keep it honest with your analytics.  
12. Keep testing copy, creative, audiences, and targeting, and retire the weak combinations.

One sentence the handbook uses is worth keeping because it is a gate: **advertising is not a contest of nerve. It is a contest of willingness to spend on what you can actually stand behind.**

## Two Shapes, Side by Side Again

I return to the two recaps from Chapter 1 because this is where people try to steal the ending and skip the method.

**The search recap that finished in the black, on paper.** About a month and a bit, about **2,159 HKD** spent, about **2,823 HKD** attributed, **API cost excluded**. What is copyable is the scar about the coupon, the already-ordering countries, the modest intent phrases, the click start, the tracking, the five-or-so distinct purchasers before a goal switch, the daily ceiling, and the refusal to panic-kill an empty stretch. What is not copyable is the ratio. A different product, a different week, or the same product with API cost included could flip the sign.

**The community recap that won the screenshot.** Three Reddit Ads rounds for an apology / unsent-letter tool, aimed at rooms that already talked about unsent letters. The most native-looking round had the best click-through rate and reached a payment page. **Orders: zero.** What is copyable is the diagnostic: a room is not the same as paying intent; a native tone is not the same as a will to pay; you must watch on-site behavior, not only the ads dashboard. What is not copyable is the conclusion “Reddit Ads never work.” That conclusion is just the inverse romance.

Thursday Packet can fail in either shape. It can also fail in a third: a search phrase that looks intent-heavy but belongs to a different job (people wanting a full design editor, not a Thursday export). The instrument’s job is to reveal that. The screenshot’s job is to hide it.

## Brand Terms, Competitor Terms, and Insurance

Once you have a name people might search, paid brand terms can be a kind of insurance: you would rather not lose your own door to a lookalike or to a competitor who is happy to stand in your doorway. The handbook’s traffic volume treats defense as the first duty — hold your name and your domain — and treats offensive bidding on someone else’s name as a policy-and-reputation risk you own if you take it. This book will not cheer that offense. If you ever consider it, read the current platform rules and decide whether the lawsuit-and-reputation surface is a Tuesday you want. Most small products have better Tuesdays.

Directories, promo calendars, and “intercept” tactics belong in the same caution bin as pulses: a window, a catching page, no substitute for a primary channel. Chapter 8 will name the failure modes. Ads cannot wash them clean.

## Where “Ads as a Clock” Falsifies Itself

The clock metaphor can rot.

It fails when “we are buying time” becomes a poetic way to spend after you already know the journey leaks. A clock that measures a hole is not a reason to enlarge the hole.

It fails when “restraint” becomes a way never to start, even with a green tracking card, a completable loop, and a primary room that has already done its part. Caution is a ceiling and a question. It is not a vow of poverty.

It fails when the only success you will accept is a pretty ratio, like the 2,159 / 2,823 shape. A campaign that spends a modest ceiling and proves the phrases belong to a different job can be a successful clock. You bought a no. That is time well purchased if you stop.

It fails when community ads and search ads are averaged into one “paid performance” story. They often bring different people. Chapter 7 will keep them apart. This chapter only forbids the blender.

## Creative Fatigue Is a Ceiling, Not a Mystery

Even a well-chosen phrase or a useful community ad will tire. People stop seeing it, or they start seeing it as furniture. The official hygiene list already asked you to cap frequency on display and video. Search has a quieter version of the same wear: a headline that was sharp in week one becomes wallpaper in week four, and you will misread the decline as “the market cooled.”

**Risks**

1. Doubling spend on a tired creative because “it used to work.”  
2. Changing everything at once — phrase, headline, landing, bid — so you cannot tell what broke.  
3. Treating fatigue as proof that paid acquisition is over, or as proof that you must open a new network.

**Suggestion.** Write, on the cold-start card, a simple rotation rule: how long a headline may run before a challenger is tested, and what you will hold constant while you test it. One change at a time is slow in a slide and fast in a log. If a community placement is the format, fatigue arrives sooner; native-looking copy that never changes is how a room learns to skip you.

**Conclusion.** Time, when you buy it, includes time for a message to age. Budget for that aging. Do not interpret aging as a verdict on the job.

## Takeaway

**Rule:** Ads buy time and a faster signal. They do not buy a verdict on the job.

**This week:** Write the question spend is allowed to answer. Claim the coupon in the official order. Set geography, intent phrases, and a ceiling. Do not spend until tracking can accept a purchase.

**Not a promise:** The handbook’s 2,159 / 2,823 HKD stretch (API cost excluded), daily ceiling sketches, and “about five purchasers” timing are teaching shapes, not your plan.
