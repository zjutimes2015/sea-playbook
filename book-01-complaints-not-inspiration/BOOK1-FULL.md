## Promise

After you finish, you should be able to run a **Complaint Ledger** hard enough to reject most fake demand in a week and put **one survivor** through a priced pre-sale (or a clean, dated kill).

That is a skill promise: artifacts, gates, and a verdict. It is not a money promise.

## Not a Promise

Nothing in this book guarantees MRR, ranking, traffic, or a revenue tier.

Handbook teaching cases you will meet—roughly **$10k MRR** repair-path stories, roughly **eight** backlinks with keyword difficulty near **zero** and about **41K** monthly visits—are **illustrations of shape**. Study the shape. Do not tattoo the numbers on your OKRs. Do not quote them as what *your* product will earn.

If a sentence ever sounds like certainty for sale, reread this page. Certainty is inspiration’s favorite costume.

## The Rule You Can Say Out Loud

Inspiration is a seed.  
A complaint is evidence.  
A payment is a verdict.

Keep the three layers separate. Seeds are allowed. Evidence is logged. Verdicts require money—or an honest silence after a priced ask.

## Gatekeeper Rules (Use Before You Praise)

Every go / no-go call in this series follows the same order:

1. **Risks / holes first** — at least three, concrete.  
2. **Suggestions** — what would reduce those risks.  
3. **Conclusion** — pass / conditional pass / reject, with conditions named.

Praise before risks is how Inspiration Pool meetings survive. Risks before conclusions is how Complaint Ledger meetings earn the right to spend calendar.

### Gate checklist (copy into reviews)

```text
【Risks / holes】(≥3, write first)
1. …
2. …
3. …

【Suggestions】
- …

【Conclusion】
- Pass / Conditional pass / Reject
- Conditions (if any): verification metric, open-source note, MVP scope, traffic hypothesis
```

If someone refuses to write risks first, they are asking you to fund a vibe. Decline politely. Decline in writing.

# Chapter 1 — The Inspiration Wall

The whiteboard’s top-right column said **Inspiration Pool**.

Yellow sticky notes covered half the wall. Someone had written “AI journal app.” Someone else: “Clone X, add three differentiators.” A third: “The market *must* need this.” The product lead was already estimating weeks. Engineering was counting endpoints. The founder’s finger hovered over the word **Approve** for three long seconds.

In those three seconds, the room was arguing about the wrong thing.

They were asking whether a brain-invented idea should count as a demand that already existed.

Get that wrong, and the next few months of code become polite spinning.

I have sat in that room—sometimes as the person with the sticky note, sometimes as the person who should have said no. The sticky notes feel productive. They photograph well. They fit a slide titled “Innovation.” A folder of one-star reviews does not. Neither does a Reddit thread where strangers call your favorite workflow “hell.”

So teams optimize for the photogenic version of discovery.

This book is about the other version.

**Finding demand is not finding inspiration.** Ideas are cheap. What is scarce is the judgment to stand at the source of real pain—and the discipline to treat that pain as a hypothesis until money moves.

## The Three-Second Tax

Those three seconds on Approve are expensive because they feel free.

No invoice arrives when you nod. No alert fires when a sticky note becomes a ticket. The tax shows up later as calendar debt: weeks of polite spinning, a launch that “went fine,” a quiet chart, and a new workshop to invent the next inspiration. Teams rarely connect the tax to the three seconds. They connect it to “market timing” or “onboarding” or “we needed more AI.”

A Complaint Ledger is a way to front-load a cheaper tax. Logging twenty structured complaints costs an afternoon. Killing a hypothesis costs pride. Both are cheaper than a quarter of endpoints nobody hired.

You do not need to become cynical about ideas. You need to become strict about when an idea is allowed to spend other people’s time—including your future self’s time.

## Two Quiet Paths (Cases, Not Promises)

Before we turn the rule into a method, hold two honest paths from the same handbook I keep on my desk when shipping for overseas markets. Both are *cases*, not forecasts. Neither guarantees you a revenue line.

**Path one — fix what people already curse.**

In indie-builder circles, a familiar pattern shows up again and again at roughly the **$10k MRR** scale: the product was not a brand-new category invented in a brainstorm. It was a repair job. Users were already using *something*. They hit the same friction on a schedule. The current fix still failed. The builder who won did not invent a need; they closed a gap that showed up as repeated complaints—bad reviews, “is there a tool for this?” posts, plugin one-stars, Discord rants with receipts.

Read that path for the discipline, not the number. The discipline is: enter from complaints, confirm scene + frequency + gap, then ask whether you can close the gap cheaper than the current mess. The number is someone else’s scoreboard. It is not a promise that your repair job will land at the same altitude.

### A note on “I already know my users”

Solo builders often skip the wall of stickies and still invent indoors. You live in the problem; you assume that counts as outside. Sometimes it does—especially if you are the daily user of the painful workflow. Often it does not, because your taste, your skill, and your willingness to tolerate ugliness are not a market.

Use yourself as a *generator* of candidate complaints. Use strangers as the *filter*. If the only person who repeats your pain is you, you may have a personal script, not a product. Personal scripts can still become products—after a pre-sale or a small cohort of people who do not share your Slack.


**Path two — when search intent starts to look like evidence.**

Another pattern looks like inspiration from far away and like measured intent up close: a brutally simple page, almost no backlinks (handbook case: about **eight**), keyword difficulty near **zero**, and traffic in the neighborhood of **~41K** monthly visits with roughly sixty percent from search. The lesson is not “copy this keyword and get rich.” The lesson is narrower and harder: when people outside your building already search and ask in loops, your sticky note finally has a chance to graduate from vibe to evidence. Scene-words and model-words still die young. Long-term assets still need a product loop and a brand that survives the next hype wave.

Put the two paths next to each other and the room gets quieter:

**What demand work actually lacks is not idea count. It is judgment at the source of real pain.**

The Inspiration Pool is loud and flattering. The Complaint Pool is loud, dirty, and ugly in a meeting deck. The second one looks more like a business entrance.

## What a Complaint Is Doing (That a Sticky Note Is Not)

A useful complaint is often three signals glued together:

1. The person is already using *something* (the scene is real).  
2. They hit the problem on a schedule (frequency, not a one-off mood).  
3. The current fix still fails (a gap you might close cheaper).

An inspiration sticky usually has none of those. It has vibes. It has a competitor logo with three “differentiators” taped underneath. It has a sentence that starts with “I feel like the market…”—which is English for “I have not checked.”

Treat inspiration as a seed. Seeds are fine. Seeds are how forests start. Seeds are also how you waste a season if you plant them in concrete and call the concrete a market.

Steve Blank’s old line still earns its keep. In *The Startup Owner’s Manual* and the customer-development material around it, the rule is stated plainly:

> There are no facts inside your building, so get outside.

The brainstorm is opinion with better lighting. Repeated complaints, bad reviews, and “is there a tool for this?” posts are the raw ore of facts. Ore is not yet a product. But it is closer to evidence than a wall of yellow paper.

### What “ore” still is not

Ore is not a roadmap. Ore is not a pricing page. Ore is not permission to skip GitHub. Treating complaints as automatic green lights recreates Inspiration Pool with uglier handwriting.

The honest sequence looks slower on a whiteboard photo and faster on a year chart:

1. Collect ore (structured complaints).  
2. Cluster into themes.  
3. Write one-sentence hypotheses.  
4. Kill what cannot show payment clues or a clear gap.  
5. Fire-test what remains.  
6. Sell air before heavy build.  
7. Only then estimate endpoints.

Chapters 4–7 walk steps 5–6 in detail. Chapter 1 only needs you to stop pretending step 0—the sticky note—was already step 7.


You will meet people who say they “already talk to customers.” Sometimes they do. Sometimes they talk to friends who say “sounds great,” which is not outside—it is a softer inside. Chapter 3 will give you a ledger that refuses that softness. For now, keep the distinction: **outside** means people who do not owe you encouragement.

## Inspiration Engineering vs. Complaint Engineering

Same whiteboard. Two ways to run the meeting. Completely different organizational motion.

When you treat discovery as **inspiration engineering**, you ask:

- Is the idea flashy enough?
- Can we write three differentiators into a deck?
- Will the feature list survive a three-month roadmap?

When you treat discovery as **complaint engineering**, you ask:

1. Whose hair is on fire, in what scene, and what is broken about the current fix—expensive, hard, heavy, missing the scene, or unstable?
2. Does this complaint repeat across posts, platforms, and weeks? (A single rant is not diligence; it is a mood.)
3. Is there a payment clue—someone saying they would pay, a competitor already collecting money, a pre-sale that moves cash? Likes and surveys are weak signals.
4. Is there already an open-source answer on GitHub? What is the gap? Why build instead of use?

The first set of questions pushes you to fill the wall and start coding. The second set pushes you to run a fire test and sell air *before* you schedule heavy build. Later chapters will unpack both tools. Chapter 1 only needs you to feel the fork in the road.

There is a crude metaphor I keep because it is hard to argue with: when a user’s hair is on fire, do they grab a **broken broom** that puts the fire out now, or wait for a beautifully designed professional extinguisher? Your product is either the broom or a nice-to-have. **In front of fake demand, a perfect product is meaningless.**

That sentence is not permission to ship garbage. It is permission to stop polishing a solution nobody is trying to hire.

## How to Read Evidence Without Self-Flattery

The **~$10k MRR repair path** does not teach “fix reviews and print money.” It teaches a sequence: start from complaints and help posts; confirm already-using + frequency + gap; then ask whether you can close the gap at a cost the buyer will accept. It does not promise your project will reach that scale.

The **low-competition intent page** teaches the other half: search intent and community complaints should cross-check each other; keyword tools are sieves, not treasure chests; new words can be short feedback loops, while durable assets still need product and brand. Chasing model-word waves and chasing Inspiration Walls are the same addiction wearing different merch.

Leave half a step for the skeptic—because the skeptic is often right about *bad* listening:

If the “complaints” you harvest are memes, sarcasm, or free spectators venting with no purchase intent, you did not collect commercial signal. You collected noise. Someone can say you “listened to users” and still end up with a feature graveyard. They are half right. Bad signal is not proof that all demand methods are chicken soup. And reading a Jobs-to-Be-Done article is not a get-out-of-jail card if you still refuse to pre-sell or write risks down. Tuition still shows up on the calendar as weeks of polite spinning.

One sharper edge: many people love to complain and hate to pay. Complaint density is not payment density. That is why “sell air”—clear pre-sales with a real price, even a little high so friends cannot fake-support you—belongs in this system. A pre-sale that fails is not humiliation. It is time returned.

## Where This Framing Falsifies Itself

Say the edges out loud so “complaints, not inspiration” does not harden into a new slogan.

1. **Single-source emotion with no cross-check.** One subreddit on fire is not a trend. You still need search, landing pages, competitor revenue, or another community to collide with the same pain.
2. **Translating complaints straight into a feature list.** Without a job-to-be-done sentence, you ship a patch collage of every insult and still collect zero dollars.
3. **Skipping open-source comparison.** “I can build it” is not “it is worth building.”
4. **Endless “one more week of signals” after a gate should have closed.** Gates exist so you can take a small step, not so you never leave the building.
5. **Automation that starts posting, DMing, or farming communities.** Read-only collection is fine. Judgment and contact stay human.

If any of those five is your real habit, this book will not save you—it will only give you prettier vocabulary for the same waste.

## The Rule You Can Say Out Loud

You will not finish this chapter with a billion-dollar idea. You will finish with a rule you can say in the next meeting without sounding mystical:

> Inspiration is a seed.  
> A complaint is evidence.  
> A payment is a verdict.

Everything else in this book—the fire test, the job-to-be-done sentence, the four-source chain, selling air before you write code—is how to keep that rule from rotting into another slogan on the wall next to Inspiration Pool.

Clear the Inspiration Pool for one week. Open a Complaint Ledger instead. If the ledger stays empty, you do not have a creativity problem. You have a listening problem—or a courage problem about what you might hear.

Either way, the sticky notes can wait.

Before you approve the next feature, ask three questions that fit in the same three seconds the founder’s finger used to hover:

- **Which sentence are we testing this week?** Write it down. “I feel like…” does not count.
- **Can complaints from more than one source hold that sentence up? Where is the payment clue?** If not, collect and pre-sell before you schedule.
- **If the pre-sale is zero, did we learn—or did we get embarrassed?** Only the first answer turns demand work into an engineering practice. The second trains everyone to hide bad signals.

Inspiration will still arrive. Sticky notes can still go up.

But if this week buys you one falsified “users will want this,” one repeated review theme, and one large feature you correctly refuse to start—that week was not a loss.

What demand work should collect was never the word *inspiration*. It was the honesty that keeps you from inventing inside the building when the pain is already speaking outside.

# Chapter 2 — Why Organizations Prefer Ideas

If Chapter 1 felt obvious—“of course complaints beat sticky notes”—ask why the sticky notes keep winning anyway.

They win because organizations are not optimizing for truth. They are optimizing for something that looks like progress under fluorescent lights.

Ideas are polite. Complaints are rude. Ideas fit a deck. Complaints fit a support queue. Ideas make the founder sound visionary. Complaints make the founder sound like someone who reads one-star reviews on a Saturday night.

So the room drifts toward Inspiration Pool—not because everyone is stupid, but because the incentives are aligned against hearing what hurts.

---

## Solo Founders Have Organizations Too

You might be reading this as a one-person company and thinking incentive talk is for corporates.

You still have an organization. It has one employee who likes feeling productive, one investor who is also you, and one customer-success team that would rather ship a feature than sit with unread one-star reviews. The org chart fits on a sticky note. The misalignment still fits in your calendar.

Solo incentive drift looks like this:

- You reward yourself for commits, not for kills.  
- You announce builds on Twitter/X because announcements feel like progress.  
- You avoid priced conversations because rejection feels personal when the brand is your name.  
- You call a weekend of brainstorming “strategy.”

Blank’s outside still applies. Your apartment is the building. Your head is the densest conference room on earth. Getting outside might mean ten stranger threads and one payment link—not a field trip with a clipboard.

## A Meeting That Looked Like Output

Picture a Monday standup that goes well on paper.

Design shares three concepts. Product adds six tickets. Engineering estimates fourteen points. Marketing drafts a launch thread. Someone pastes a competitor screenshot with a red circle around a missing button. The board fills. The sprint looks full. The team feels busy in the way that photographs as “shipping culture.”

Nobody opened the folder of reviews tagged “billing is a nightmare.”
Nobody pasted the Reddit thread where power users describe a workaround that takes forty minutes.
Nobody asked whether the six tickets were hired to finish a job a stranger would pay for—or hired to finish a story the team told itself last Thursday.

By Friday the demo works. By next quarter the retention chart does not. The postmortem invents a new inspiration: “We need better onboarding.” Another wall of stickies appears. The cycle feels rational because every step produced artifacts. Artifacts are not the same as facts.

Steve Blank again, because the sentence is a mirror you can hold up in that Monday room:

> There are no facts inside your building, so get outside.

(*The Startup Owner’s Manual* and the customer-development tradition around it.)

Inside the building you have opinions, politics, roadmap theater, and the warm glow of agreement among people who share a Slack workspace. Outside you have strangers who do not care about your sprint points. Those strangers leave traces: complaints, cancellations, “has anyone found a tool that…,” and occasional payments. Traces are ugly. Traces are also closer to the ground.

### Facts vs. consensus

Inside teams confuse consensus with fact. If five smart people agree in a huddle, the sentence feels verified. It is only verified as *shared opinion*. Outside traces can disagree with the huddle for months while the huddle keeps shipping. The ledger’s job is to make disagreement expensive to ignore: a URL, a repeat count, and an empty payment field sitting next to your beautiful consensus.

You will not always obey the ledger. Obeying every time is not the claim. The claim is that ignoring it should feel like a conscious risk—not like default Monday behavior.

## Three Incentives That Prefer Inspiration

### 1. Leadership wants a story that sounds like the future

“We have an inspiration” sounds like vision. “We have a pile of complaints” sounds like customer service. Boards and investors (and sometimes your own ambition) reward narrative momentum. A sticky note that says “AI-native workspace for creators” travels farther in a pitch than a screenshot that says “export still breaks CSV encodings and I am done.”

The trap is subtle: vision is not the enemy. Vision without a complaint ledger becomes a costume. You can wear it on stage while the product underneath is still guessing.

### 2. Managers need something that looks like delivery

A wall of stickies is legible management. A week spent structuring Reddit JSON into a table of repeated pains is illegible until someone translates it into tickets—and by then the political credit often goes to the ticket writers, not the listeners.

If your OKRs only count features shipped and dates hit, who volunteers to say: “This week we should not write code. We should kill three hypotheses and log twenty structured complaints”? That sentence sounds like underperformance in a culture that confuses motion with learning.

So people invent “innovation workshops.” Workshops produce photos. Photos produce Slack emoji. Emoji produce the feeling that discovery happened. Discovery did not happen. A calendar event happened.

### 3. Individuals are scored on visible craft

Engineers get graded on interfaces shipped. Designers get graded on flows polished. PMs get graded on roadmap clarity. Researchers—if you even have them—get graded on the page count of insight decks. Almost nobody is graded on: **Did our claimed user need survive contact with unpaid strangers and a payment link?**

Under that scoring system, inspiration is career-safe. It creates work that looks like your job. Complaints create ambiguity. Ambiguity creates the risk that your beautiful module should never have been built. Humans avoid that risk even when they quote Blank in their newsletters.

### A short autopsy of “insight theater”

I have watched—and sometimes co-authored—documents titled *User Insights Q3* that never changed a single scheduled ticket. The insights were real enough. The org had already spent the political capital on a roadmap. So the deck became ritual: proof that listening occurred, filed next to the launch plan that did not move.

Insight theater is how complaint work dies politely. If your structured complaints cannot kill or reorder work, you do not have a discovery practice. You have a content hobby attached to a build machine.

The fix is not a longer deck. The fix is a rule with teeth: **no net-new heavy build without a ledger theme and a written kill/keep decision from the last week.** If that rule is impossible in your company, document that fact. It is itself a finding.

## How “Demand Work” Gets Redefined Into Brainstorming

Watch the language drift.

Week 1: “We need to find demand.”
Week 2: “Let’s brainstorm opportunities.”
Week 3: “Let’s prioritize the idea backlog.”
Week 4: “Let’s estimate the top three.”

Somewhere between Week 1 and Week 2, **finding** was replaced by **inventing**. The verbs changed, so the ethics changed. Inventing rewards cleverness. Finding rewards patience and the stomach for bad news.

Brainstorms are not evil. They are a tool for combining known pieces into new shapes. They are a terrible primary instrument for discovering whether a pain is real, frequent, and unpaid-for by current tools. Using a brainstorm as your demand engine is like using a mirror to survey a city: you will see yourself clearly and the streets not at all.

Teams double down when inspiration feels thin. Not enough dazzle? Run another workshop. Enough dazzle? Skip to the roadmap. The missing step—the one that feels like unpaid intern work—is standing in the complaint stream long enough to see what repeats.

## What “Getting Outside” Actually Costs

Blank’s line is famous enough to become wallpaper. Wallpaper does not change calendars.

Getting outside costs three things organizations hate to budget:

**Time that does not produce a demo.** Reading forty threads, tagging sentiment, and writing “this is probably noise” is real work with nothing to show in a Friday demo. If your culture only celebrates demos, listeners lose.

**Status risk.** Reporting “users hate our favorite workflow” can sound like disloyalty to the people who built it. So bad news gets sanded into “opportunities for delight.” Delight is often a euphemism for “we are afraid to say broken.”

**The death of pet ideas.** A Complaint Ledger will kill projects that already have names, logos, and Slack channels. That feels like waste. It is the opposite of waste. The waste was going to be the next six months.

If you cannot afford those three costs, you will keep quoting “get outside” while staying in. The quote will make you sound sophisticated. The roadmap will still be fiction.

---

## Redefine the Week’s Output

Here is the practical move—small enough to try without a reorganization, sharp enough to change what gets praised.

Stop treating “features completed” as the only weekly output of a discovery-shaped team.

For one week—longer if you can defend it—define output as:

1. **Structured complaints logged** (with source links, not vibes), and  
2. **Hypotheses killed** (written down, with the evidence that killed them).

A killed hypothesis is a deliverable. It is a saved sprint. It is the adult version of innovation.

Example of a week that looks empty to an old scoreboard and rich to a new one:

- 27 complaint rows entered from two subreddits, one plugin review cluster, and a Discord help channel.
- 4 themes that repeated across sources.
- 3 themes marked as meme/sarcasm noise.
- 2 hypotheses written in one sentence each.
- 1 hypothesis killed after no payment clue and an open-source tool that already covers 80% of the job.
- 1 hypothesis kept for a fire test and a pre-sale page next week.

No endpoints shipped. More truth than most “innovation sprints.”

If your manager asks what you did, do not apologize. Show the ledger. Show the kill. Say: “We prevented a build.” If that sentence is unwelcome, you have learned something important about the organization—something no sticky note would have taught you.

---

## A One-Week Experiment You Can Run Without Permission

You do not need a reorg memo. You need seven days and a shared doc.

**Day 1:** Freeze new sticky notes for product ideas. Existing backlog can stay; no new inspiration entries.  
**Day 2–3:** Pick two vertical communities or review clusters. Log at least fifteen ledger rows (Chapter 3 template).  
**Day 4:** Cluster into themes. Write three hypotheses in one sentence each.  
**Day 5:** Kill at least one hypothesis in writing. If you cannot kill one, your bar for “alive” is too soft.  
**Day 6:** For the strongest survivor, note payment clues and open-source gaps.  
**Day 7:** Present to whoever usually approves builds: not a feature list—**complaints logged + hypotheses killed + one candidate for fire test.**

If Day 7 is laughed out of the room, you learned the incentive map. If Day 7 earns a pause on a pet project, you changed the org a little. Either outcome beats another photogenic workshop.


---

## Soft Edges (So This Chapter Does Not Become Anti-Idea Theater)

Ideas still matter. Seeds still matter. Founders who cannot imagine a better shape will only patch forever.

The claim is narrower:

**Ideas are not allowed to outrank unpaid evidence when you decide what to build next.**

Also: some teams are not “avoiding complaints” so much as drowning in unstructured venting. A #feedback channel with three thousand messages and zero tags is not a Complaint Ledger. It is a junk drawer. Chapter 3 exists because raw pain without structure becomes another kind of Inspiration Pool—chaotic, flattering to “we listen,” useless for decisions.

And one more edge: if you redefine weekly output as complaints + kills, but never schedule a pre-sale or a small build after a gate passes, you have only invented a new way to hide. Listening forever is also a status-safe behavior. Gates exist so learning can end in a decision.

---

## What to Say in the Room

When the finger hovers over Approve again, you do not need a speech. You need a swap of metrics.

Old question: “Which idea excites us?”
New question: “Which complaint theme survived cross-checks—and which hypothesis did we kill this week?”

Old artifact: photo of the Inspiration Pool.
New artifact: a ledger row with a URL, a repeat count, and a payment clue field that is either filled or honestly blank.

Old pride: “We generated forty ideas.”
New pride: “We invalidated three in five days and only one earned a fire test.”

That pride feels colder at first. It is supposed to. Cold is what outside air feels like after too long in a conference room.

---

## What Changes When Kills Become Public

Private kills are easy. You quietly drop an idea in your notes and tell yourself you were disciplined. Public kills are different. They teach the room that changing your mind with evidence is status-positive.

Try a lightweight ritual: a shared “Killed this week” section at the top of the standup doc. Two or three lines maximum. No humiliation theater—no naming who loved the idea hardest. Name the hypothesis and the evidence. Over a month, people start bringing better evidence because they do not want their pet theme to die on soft grounds. That is a healthy arms race.

If your culture turns public kills into gotchas, move the ritual into a smaller circle first. The point is not transparency cosplay. The point is making learning cheaper than defending last week’s slide.

### Roadmaps as sunk-cost machines

Once a theme has a name, a Figma file, and a channel, it acquires gravity. Gravity is not evidence. Gravity is inertia with branding.

Complaint-led teams fight gravity with a dating rule: themes do not get channels until they pass a fire test or a priced signal. Before that, they live in the ledger as rows. Rows are easy to delete. Channels are hard to delete because deletion feels like firing a colleague who happens to be imaginary.

You will still create channels too early sometimes. When you do, allow a funeral: archive the channel with a link to the kill reason. Funerals beat haunted roadmaps.


---

## Takeaway

**Organizations prefer ideas because ideas are legible, flattering, and aligned with how people are scored—not because ideas are closer to truth.**

**Blank’s rule (use it as a calendar constraint, not a quote decoration):** There are no facts inside your building, so get outside. (*The Startup Owner’s Manual* / customer development.)

**Redefine this week’s output:**
- Count **structured complaints logged** (with sources).
- Count **hypotheses killed** (with the evidence that killed them).
- Treat a prevented build as a win.


### Pair the metric with a visible kill

Counting complaints alone can become a new vanity metric—“we logged 200 pains.” Pair every week with at least one explicit kill, even a small one. Kills train the muscle organizations lack: ending work that felt promising.

Write the kill where people can see it: “Killed: AI journal for generic productivity. Reason: no repeated scene outside our heads; substitutes already include Notes apps; payment clue none.” That paragraph is more valuable than a mood board.


**Watch for:** workshops that produce photos; OKRs that only count shipped tickets; “we listen” channels with no structure; listening forever with no gate.

# Chapter 3 — The Complaint Signal

Not every complaint is a business signal.

That sentence saves more runway than most frameworks. Teams that “listen to users” without a filter end up with a product shaped like a complaint bingo card: every insult becomes a ticket, every ticket becomes a half-feature, every half-feature becomes a reason the roadmap never thins.

Chapter 1 said a useful complaint often carries three signals glued together. This chapter turns that into a filter you can run in public—and into a four-question ledger you can fill without waiting for a research team.

---

## A Thread That Looked Like Demand (and Wasn’t)

Open a vertical subreddit on a Tuesday night. Sort by comments. The top post is funny. The title is a joke about a tool everyone loves to hate. The top comment is a meme GIF. Halfway down, someone writes: “lol same.” Someone else: “I just deal with it.” One person links a competitor and says “this one is also bad.” The thread ends. Your notes app now has a line: *People hate Tool X — opportunity?*

That line is how fake demand is born: a social weather report mistaken for a purchase order.

Contrast it with another kind of thread. Title: “How are you exporting weekly client reports without breaking formulas?” The body describes a Monday morning scene, names the current stack, lists the twenty-minute workaround, and asks whether anyone pays for a fix. Three replies name the same paid workaround. Two say they hired a VA. One says they would pay “if it didn’t require another SaaS login.” The pain has a schedule. The scene is specific. The current solutions exist and still fail a named job. Now you have ore—not yet a product, but ore.

The difference is not “emotion versus calm.” Both threads can be emotional. The difference is **structure**: already using something, hitting it on a frequency, and still facing a gap.

---

## Worked Mini-Example (Composite, Not a Case Study Promise)

Suppose you skim a design-tools subreddit and a plugin store.

**Row A:** Meme post roasting a popular editor. Comments are jokes. Substitutes mentioned as punchlines. Payment clue: none. Status: `noise`.

**Row B:** Thread—“Client PDF markups break every Thursday handoff.” Body names the stack, the weekly schedule, the thirty-minute fix, and two paid tools people tried. Repeat: you find similar language in two plugin one-stars. Payment clue: people already paying for partial fixes. Open-source gap: a library exists but needs a thin workflow shell. Status: `theme` → `fire_test_next`.

**Row C:** Friend DM—“I’d use your AI markup helper, sounds great.” No scene, no frequency, no price. Status: do not enter as evidence; optional note under Seeds.

Only Row B earns the next chapter’s attention. Rows A and C taught you what to refuse. Refusals are part of signal quality.

This composite is a teaching sketch, not a claim that a markup helper will hit any revenue number. The point is the sorting, not the niche.


---

## The Three-Part Signal

Treat a complaint as a candidate signal only when all three parts show up—or when you can honestly mark which part is missing.

### 1. Already using something (the scene is real)

If nobody is doing the job today with a tool, a spreadsheet, a VA, or a painful ritual, you may be looking at a fantasy job. Fantasies can become markets later. They are terrible first bets for a small team.

“Already using” does not mean “using a direct competitor.” It can mean duct tape: Notion + Zapier + a Google Sheet named `FINAL_v7_really`. Duct tape is evidence. It means the job is happening without you.

Ask: **What do they grab when the problem appears?** If the answer is “nothing, they just wish,” keep digging or walk away. Wishes are inspiration wearing a sad face.

### 2. Frequency (not a one-off mood)

A single bad day is a story. A weekly collision is a system.

Frequency can be calendar (“every Monday export”), event-driven (“every time we onboard a client”), or scale-driven (“fine for one shop, collapses at ten”). What matters is that the pain is scheduled by reality, not by your interview calendar.

Anti-pattern: sampling only launch-week rage or a viral dunk. Virality measures shareability. Shareability is not frequency.

### 3. A gap (the current fix still fails)

If the current fix works well enough that buyers shrug, you do not have a gap—you have a preference. Preferences can support brands with huge distribution. They rarely support a first product from a small builder unless you have a unfair channel.

Gaps look like: too expensive, too hard, too heavy, missing the scene, unstable, or requiring a human who does not scale. Write the gap in words a stranger would recognize. “Not delightful” is not a gap. “CSV export corrupts non-ASCII client names every Friday” is a gap.

### Writing the gap so a stranger would nod

Bad gap: “UX is outdated.”  
Better gap: “On a 12-inch laptop, the approve button sits under a sticky footer, so managers approve the wrong version twice a week.”

Bad gap: “Too expensive.”  
Better gap: “Team of three pays for a 50-seat plan because billing only comes in enterprise packs.”

Bad gap: “Needs AI.”  
Better gap: “They paste the same client brief into a chat tool every Monday and re-type constraints the tool forgot.”

Concrete gaps can be tested. Aesthetic gaps turn into endless redesign inspiration.


When all three parts are present, you are allowed to get interested. Interested is not the same as building. Interested means: log it, cross-check it, and run it toward a fire test later.

---

## Anti-Patterns: What Looks Like Signal and Isn’t

### Meme and sarcasm density

Communities perform hatred as entertainment. “This software is the devil” under a meme can mean “we bond by roasting,” not “we will pay for an alternative tomorrow.” Mark meme-heavy threads as low confidence. If the same concrete failure mode appears later in non-meme posts, upgrade it. If it only lives as a joke, leave it.

### The single rant as a project brief

One eloquent angry user can sound like a market. They are a sample size of one with good prose. Eloquence is not replication. Require repeats across posts, accounts, or platforms before a rant becomes a theme. A theme can still die in pre-sale. That is fine. A theme is still more honest than a soliloquy.

### Friend “sounds great”

Friends and friendly founders will support your identity. They will say the sentence that keeps dinner pleasant. Price the product in front of them—real number, real payment link—and watch how often “sounds great” fails to become a charge. Keep friends for morale. Keep strangers for verdicts.

Related weak signals: survey checkboxes, waitlist emails with no price, and internal Slack polls among people who already share your worldview. Use them as whispers. Do not use them as gates.

### Feature bingo from unbounded venting

“Listening” without a job-to-be-done frame turns into a list of parts: dark mode, CSV, SSO, mobile, “make it more AI.” You can ship all of it and still not be hired for a task. Chapter 5 will tighten the job sentence. For now: if you cannot say what progress the user is trying to make in a scene, you are collecting parts, not demand.

### Indie-only echo chambers

If every complaint you collect is from other indie hackers about tools for indie hackers, you may be farming an inner loop. Inner loops feel like community. They often lack the cashflows of boring industries with dirty work. You can still build for builders—just do it with open eyes, not because that was the only Discord you joined.

---

## The Four-Question Ledger

When a thread, review cluster, or help post survives a first glance, do not paste it into a motivational note. Run four questions. Write the answers in a ledger row. Empty fields are allowed. Empty fields are information.

**Q1. What are the top concentrated complaints?**  
Name up to three. Use the user’s words where you can. Concentration beats novelty. One boring repeated complaint outranks five exotic ones.

**Q2. What substitutes get mentioned most?**  
Tools, humans, workflows, “I just do it manually.” Substitutes reveal the real competitive set—including doing nothing automated.

**Q3. What tool-shaped opportunities appear?**  
Not a full product vision. A wedge: the smallest useful fix that would remove the Monday fire. If the only opportunity you can name is “rebuild the whole category,” you are still in inspiration mode.

**Q4. What payment clues exist?**  
Phrases like “I’d pay for,” existing paid tools, hiring a VA, agency invoices, or a competitor’s pricing page that people already tolerate. No clue? Mark `payment_clue: none yet`. That is not failure. That is a stop sign until a pre-sale or another source supplies one.

These four questions are the same spine as the Reddit/JSON analysis prompt many overseas builders already use. The ledger simply makes them durable across sources—plugin reviews, Discord, navigation-site request boards, support mail—so you are not reinventing the ritual each time.

---

## From JSON Prompt to Habit

If you pull Reddit (or similar) structured data, the four questions map cleanly to a repeatable prompt many builders already keep:

1. What are the users’ top 3 concentrated complaints?  
2. Which substitute products get mentioned most?  
3. What new tool opportunities appear?  
4. What clues suggest willingness to pay?

The habit is not the AI call. The habit is **writing answers into durable rows** with URLs and risks. Models help compress. You still decide what is meme, what is theme, and what dies.

Automate listening and first-pass tags if you want. Do not automate posting, DMing, or “engagement.” Communities notice. Credit is expensive to rebuild. Read-only collection stays on the right side of the line Chapter 8 will draw in harder ink.


Add fields that keep you honest:

- **Source URL** (or archive reference)
- **Repeat count** (how many independent times you’ve seen the theme)
- **Paid clue** (quote or link; or explicit none)
- **Open-source gap** (what exists on GitHub / why not just use it)
- **Risk** (at least one reason this row might be noise)

If you cannot fill source URL and a risk line, you are not logging a complaint. You are journaling.

### Payment clue field ethics

Do not invent willingness-to-pay from tone. “This is frustrating” is not “I will buy.” Prefer quotes, linked pricing of substitutes, VA/agency spend, or your own priced test results. If you catch yourself writing `paid_clue: probably`—delete it. Probably is inspiration with a spreadsheet hat.

When a real clue appears, copy enough context that a teammate can audit you without re-finding the thread cold. Auditability is how ledgers stay non-political.

A printable version lives in this book’s templates folder as `complaint-ledger.md`. Steal it. Change the columns if you must. Do not delete the payment clue or risk fields to make the sheet feel more optimistic.

---

## How a Row Becomes a Theme (and When to Stop)

One row is a hint.
Three rows with the same concentrated complaint across sources is a theme.
A theme plus a payment clue is a candidate for the fire test (Chapter 4).
A theme with only meme energy and no substitutes is entertainment.

### Cross-source minimum

Before you call something a theme, try to see it in more than one “species” of source: for example, a community thread *and* a store review, or a help-desk tag *and* a competitor’s pricing-page complaint. Two posts in the same meme-friendly thread can be one mood with multiple avatars. Two species reduce the chance you are measuring a local joke.

If you only have one species after an honest search, keep the row on `watch`. Watching is allowed. Building from a single species is how Inspiration Pool sneaks back in through a side door labeled “qualitative insight.”


Stopping rules matter as much as starting rules:

- Stop collecting for a theme when new rows stop changing the top three complaints.
- Stop pretending a theme is strong when every payment clue field is empty after a deliberate look at substitutes and competitor pricing.
- Stop translating the theme into a six-month build. The next honest step is still validation, not architecture.

---

## Light Self-Falsification

This chapter’s filter fails in predictable ways. Name them so you catch yourself.

**If you treat the three-part test as a vibe check instead of written fields,** you will smuggle inspiration back in. “It *feels* like they already use something” is not “already using.”

**If you optimize the ledger for volume,** you will hire yourself as a librarian of pain and never decide. Count themes and kills, not only rows.

**If you fill payment clues with your own willingness to pay,** you are surveying a sample of one founder. Your wallet is a hint, not a market.

**If you skip open-source comparison because “ours will be cleaner,”** you may be about to rebuild a wheel with better CSS and worse distribution.

**If the only sources you trust are English mega-subreddits,** you will miss local payment habits and overfit to people who enjoy posting. For overseas products, country distribution and the communities where buyers actually speak still matter—complaint signal is not only Reddit-shaped.

---

## Back to the Two Threads

The meme thread taught bonding. The export thread taught a Monday job.

Your job as a demand worker is not to harvest every feeling. It is to notice which feelings arrive with a scene, a schedule, and a gap—and to write that down in a form that can be killed next week without drama.

Inspiration can still sit in a side column labeled Seeds. Seeds are allowed. Seeds do not get a sprint until they pass through the ledger and pick up evidence like burs on a sweater.

When someone asks in the meeting, “But users are complaining—shouldn’t we build?”, you finally have a non-mystical answer:

“Show me the three-part signal. Show me four ledger answers. Show me a payment clue that is not our friends. Then we talk about building.”

If they cannot, you are not being difficult. You are being the adult who refuses to confuse noise with ore.

## Field Craft: Collecting Complaints Without Cosplay

Chapter 3’s filter is useless if your intake is lazy. This section is the operational depth: how to pull ore from ponds builders actually use—especially Reddit-shaped communities—without turning “listening” into a second full-time job or a spam career.

### Pick a vertical pond (not the entire internet)

Write one sentence: **who already pays for adjacent work**, and **where they talk when stuck**. Examples of pond types (not commandments):

- A vertical subreddit or forum where the job shows up weekly  
- A plugin / extension / theme store niche with downloads *and* one-stars  
- A request board where people write “I want…” in plain language  
- A competitor’s community you can **listen** inside (not spray)  
- Your own support tags, if you already have users  

If you cannot name why speakers in that pond can pay—even roughly—pick another pond. Tourist ponds produce eloquent poverty.

Cap yourself: **one primary pond, one secondary for cross-check.** Twelve tabs is inspiration theater with better bookmarks.

### Reddit-shaped intake (read-only)

A common overseas-builder ritual:

1. Enter a vertical community where buyers already speak.  
2. Prefer help / rant / “is there a tool” posts with real scenes over joke megathreads.  
3. When the platform offers machine-readable thread data (for example appending a structured JSON view to a thread URL on Reddit), pull it for analysis—not for auto-replies.  
4. Run the four questions.  
5. Write durable ledger rows with URLs and risks.  
6. **Stop.** Do not auto-comment, auto-DM, or “engage to build authority” with scripts.

Reusable analysis spine (same as the ledger):

```text
Analyze the thread/cluster data:
1. Top 3 concentrated complaints (user language)
2. Substitutes mentioned most
3. Tool-shaped wedge opportunities (smallest broom, not category reboot)
4. Payment clues (quotes, paid tools, VA/agency spend) — or explicit none
```

AI can draft the four answers. You still mark `noise` vs `theme`, update `repeat_count`, and refuse to invent `paid_clue`.

### What “good raw capture” looks like on Day 1

Aim for **10–20 raw artifacts** before you solutioneer:

- Title + URL  
- Ugly quote that carries scene language  
- Date captured  
- Pond name  

Do not capture only the cleverest lines. Clever is a bias. Capture the boring weekly collisions.

If after twenty artifacts you still have zero rows with all three signal parts even partially marked, you may be in a meme pond. Change ponds before you change product ideas.

### Plugin and store reviews

High downloads + low stars is often richer than a viral roast. Reviews tend to name the gap in operational language: export fails, billing packs, “broke after update,” “support never answered,” “works until client count > N.”

Cluster reviews by **failure mode**, not by star count alone. Five one-stars about the same Thursday export beat twenty one-stars about unrelated aesthetics.

Log the product name, review date window, and whether reviewers mention paying alternatives. That last bit feeds Q4.

### Request boards and “I want” pools

Some navigation and directory sites publish explicit request pools—people writing what they wish existed. Treat these as **pain drafts**, not purchase orders. Many requests are curiosity. Promote only when you can attach frequency, substitutes, and a payment clue from somewhere else—or when you are ready to price a test against that wording.

### Discord and live communities

Listening in competitor or niche Discords can surface scene language fast. Rules:

- Read more than you speak.  
- Do not scrape private spaces you are not allowed to.  
- Do not pitch inside help channels on day one.  
- If you later sell air, disclose and follow the community’s rules. Ban evasion is not a growth hack; it is how you lose the vein.

Member count is heat, not demand. A huge server full of lurkers can be quieter commercially than a small forum with invoices in the open.

### Deduping without killing signal

Same URL twice → one row.  
Same complaint, different species (thread + store review) → raise `repeat_count`, keep both sources listed.  
Same meme recycled weekly → do not inflate repeats; mark entertainment.

A simple rule: **repeats count independent speakers and independent surfaces**, not upvote velocity.

### When to bring a second measuring stick early

If your pond is emotionally loud but commercially mute, peek at market behavior without turning Book 1 into an SEO manual:

- Are freelancers selling the job as a service?  
- Do substitutes already charge?  
- Does search language look like a job (“how to export client markup weekly”) rather than pure brand navigation?

Write what you find under honesty fields. Then return to the ledger. Charts do not replace complaints; they stress-test them.

### Time boxing collection

| Block | Work | Stop when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 45–90 min | Raw capture in primary pond | 10–20 artifacts or diminishing new language |
| 30–45 min | Four questions on top clusters | ≥2 candidate themes or a written “pond may be wrong” |
| 20 min | Secondary pond cross-check | Confirm, weaken, or kill top theme |
| 15 min | Hygiene | Dedupe, kills, `none yet` aging |

If collection regularly eats your build week, you reinvented Failure Mode 4 (eternal digging) with a prettier spreadsheet.

### Ethics and reputation

Complaint mining is not entitlement to people’s time. You are reading public frustration to decide whether *you* should spend *your* calendar. That does not grant you the right to harvest emails, mass-DM sufferers, or impersonate support.

When you later ask for money, you enter as a vendor with a clear promise—not as a ghost who mined their pain and vanished into a pitch deck.

### Composite capture log (teaching sketch)

```text
Pond: design-ops adjacent forum + plugin reviews
Captured: 16 artifacts
Noise: 7 (meme / “lol same” / no scene)
Watch: 4 (scene but single-species)
Theme candidates: 2
  - Thursday markup export corruption (3 sources, VA mention)
  - “AI rewrite my comments” (1 species, no payment clue)
Kills today: AI rewrite theme → killed (inspiration costume; no schedule; no paid substitute fossil)
Next: fire-test export theme only
```

This sketch is not a niche recommendation. It shows what a non-fog afternoon produces: numbers, kills, and a next gate—not a mood.

## Takeaway

**Complaint signal = already using + frequency + gap.** Missing a part? Log the hole; do not pretend completeness.

**Reject as primary evidence:** meme/sarcasm piles, single rants, friend “sounds great,” waitlists with no price, feature bingo without a job.

**Ledger four questions:**
1. Top concentrated complaints (≤3)
2. Substitutes mentioned most
3. Tool-shaped wedge opportunities
4. Payment clues (or explicit none)

**Honesty fields:** source URL, repeat count, paid clue, open-source gap, risk.

**Template:** `templates/complaint-ledger.md` in this book’s folder.


### Weekly hygiene (fifteen minutes)

- Dedupe rows that point at the same URL.  
- Update `repeat_count` on themes that reappeared.  
- Force at least one `killed` or `noise` promotion so the sheet does not only grow hopeful rows.  
- Highlight any theme with `paid_clue: none yet` older than seven days—either hunt a clue, schedule a priced test, or demote.

Hygiene is boring. Boring is how ledgers stay trustworthy.

# Chapter 4 — The Fire Test

A complaint can be true and still not be a must-have.

That sentence is the adult sibling of Chapter 3. The ledger taught you to recognize ore. The fire test teaches you which ore is worth carrying out of the mine—and which ore is just interesting rocks that will look good in a slide deck while your calendar burns.

Picture a person whose hair is on fire. On the shelf sits a beautiful extinguisher: matte finish, excellent copy, a feature matrix that would make a conference booth proud. On the floor sits a broken broom. Which one do they grab?

They grab the broom.

Not because the broom is elegant. Because the job is *stop burning now*, not *experience premium fire safety*. If your product is the pretty extinguisher for a problem that is not on fire, you are building nice-to-have. Nice-to-have can still become a business—with distribution, brand, and patience most early builders do not have. Must-have is the broken broom: ugly, immediate, hired under pressure.

This chapter is a gate. Themes that survive Chapter 3 still have to answer: **when the fire shows up, do people grab something like you—or do they shrug and live with the smoke?**

Fail the gate, and you are forbidden from “serious” development. You may keep listening. You may keep logging. You may not open a six-week endpoint festival.

---

## Must-Have vs Nice-to-Have (Without Mysticism)

Must-have is not “users said they love the idea in a workshop.” Must-have is behavior under constraint.

Ask in writing—out loud if you have to—before anyone opens Figma:

1. **Who is on fire?** Name a role and a scene, not a persona adjective (“busy millennials” is not a scene).
2. **What is the fire?** One sentence a stranger would recognize without your glossary.
3. **What do they grab today?** Tool, human, ritual, spreadsheet, or “suffer quietly.”
4. **What breaks if they do nothing for another week?** If the honest answer is “mild annoyance,” you likely have nice-to-have.
5. **Would they accept an ugly first version that stops the fire?** If they only want delight, you are not in broom territory yet.

Nice-to-have often wears must-have clothing: polished decks, “everyone hates X,” a competitor with pretty UI, a founder story that makes investors nod. The tell is delayed grabbing. People bookmark your landing page. They say “interesting.” They forward it to a Slack channel named `#random`. They do not rearrange Monday morning for you.

Must-have rearranges Mondays.

### The three-second cousin

Chapter 1’s Approve tax and the fire test are cousins. Approve spends calendar on inspiration. The fire test spends pride on honesty: admitting your favorite theme is a nicer broom for a room that is not burning. Both taxes feel optional in the moment. Both show up later as polite spinning.

### “Pain” is not the same as “fire”

Pain can be chronic and still optional. People complain about email. Most will not hire a weird new inbox on Tuesday unless a scene forces the hire—a client SLA, a compliance deadline, a weekly ritual that fails in public. The fire test is picky on purpose. Chronic grumbling without a grab-moment is how you invent therapy products for markets that wanted a calendar fix.

Write the grab-moment. If you cannot, you may still have a complaint signal. You do not yet have a must-have.

---

## Four Shapes of Must-Have

Use these as lenses, not as a scavenger hunt that forces every theme into all four. One clear shape beats four forced checkmarks.

### 1. High-frequency pain

The collision happens on a schedule: daily export, weekly client handoff, every onboarding call, every payroll Friday. Retention has a natural ally—the calendar. If the pain is real and weekly, even a thin wedge can earn a habit because reality keeps booking the meeting.

**Watch for:** confusing “I saw three posts this month” with frequency. Frequency lives in the user’s life, not in your scroll speed. Also watch for seasonal fake-frequency: tax week is loud; August may be silent. Note seasonality in the ledger so you do not mistake a calendar spike for a forever fire.

### 2. Scale disaster

One shop can duct-tape it. Ten shops cannot. A freelancer survives with a spreadsheet named `FINAL_v7`; a five-person agency melts when three clients share the same fragile ritual. Products that systematize the collapse often sell as subscriptions because the alternative is hiring chaos—or becoming the chaos.

**Watch for:** building the enterprise cathedral before anyone admits the spreadsheet is on fire. Sell the smallest system that prevents the known collapse. Scale disasters tempt “platform” language. Platforms are how nice-to-have sneaks in through architecture.

### 3. Dirty work

Humans must do it and hate it: invoices, cold outreach first drafts, resume screening, CSV babysitting, compliance screenshots, chasing approvals across three tools. “Pain transfer” prices higher than “delight transfer.” People pay to make the ugliness someone else’s problem—including software’s problem.

**Watch for:** romanticizing dirty work into a lifestyle brand. Dirty work sells because it is dirty. Keep the copy concrete. If your landing page sounds like a wellness retreat, you may have polished away the fire.

### 4. Someone already pays

Wallet votes beat blank-market fantasies. Agencies on freelance marketplaces, seats on a clunky SaaS, VAs doing the job at a visible hourly rate, “we hired a contractor just for Thursday exports”—these are payment fossils. Dig them up. Your job is not always to invent a category. Often it is to productize a paid mess.

**Watch for:** “the market is empty” as a brag. Empty can mean no demand. Occupied-and-hated is often healthier ore. Also watch for mistaking *your* willingness to pay for a market. Founder wallets are hints, not panels.

A theme can wear more than one shape. Write the primary shape in the ledger. If you cannot name one without squinting, you may still have a complaint—just not a fire.

## Worked Pass / Fail (Composites, Not Promises)

These are teaching sketches. They do not forecast revenue. Handbook numbers you have seen elsewhere in this series—roughly **$10k MRR** repair stories, roughly **eight** backlinks with traffic near **41K**—remain cases, not targets to paste into your OKRs.

**Pass — Thursday markup hell.**  
Agency managers already use a PDF tool. Every Thursday handoff corrupts comments. Two paid substitutes are named in threads; both fail non-ASCII client names. People hire freelancers the week of a launch. Fire shape: high-frequency + dirty work + already paying. Ugly wedge: “fix the Thursday export without another login.” Decision: **PASS → priced test allowed**. Forbidden scope: full “document OS,” AI rewrite suite, multiplayer whiteboard.

**Fail — prettier AI journal.**  
Threads say journaling is hard. Substitutes: Notes, Notion, paper. Payment clues: free apps and “I should be more disciplined.” Fire shape: none clear; the story is delight + identity. People would wait for a beautiful extinguisher. Decision: **FAIL → kill or demote to Seeds**. A perfect product would not matter here; the fire is optional.

**Borderline — team emoji pack for Notion.**  
Complaints exist; frequency is low; payment looks tip-jar shaped. Borderline cases do not get endpoints. They get a tiny priced page with a real number—or a firm kill. Indecision is how nice-to-have eats a quarter while everyone “keeps an eye on it.”

**Pass with a scar — invoice chasing for freelancers.**  
Already using Stripe + spreadsheet + awkward email. Weekly pain. Dirty work. People pay VAs. Open-source gap: libraries exist; workflow shell does not. PASS, but write the scar: support load and payment-provider policy risk. Scars belong on the page. Hidden scars become surprise roadmaps.

## The One-Page Fire Test

Copy this into a note before anyone estimates tickets. Fill risks first. If that feels unnatural, good—you have been practicing inspiration order.

```text

1) Who is on fire (role + scene)?
2) What is the fire (one concrete sentence)?
3) What do they grab today?
4) Cost of doing nothing for 7 more days?
5) Primary must-have shape: frequency | scale | dirty | already_pays | none
6) Would they accept an ugly broom that stops the fire? (yes/no + why)
7) Cross-check: ≥2 independent sources? payment clue? open-source gap checked?
8) Risks / holes (≥3 lines — write these FIRST):
   - 
   - 
   - 
9) Decision: PASS (priced test next) | FAIL (kill/demote) | MORE SIGNAL (no build)
10) If PASS: smallest broom (one sentence). Forbidden scope (what you will not build yet):
```

**Rule:** no PASS without three written risks. Optimism is not a risk register. “We can figure it out” is not a risk line.

Template file: `templates/fire-test.md`.

### What MORE SIGNAL means

MORE SIGNAL is not a soft PASS. It means you are not allowed to pretend the gate vanished. You go back to communities, reviews, or a short priced probe that does not require a product. If two weeks later you still cannot improve the page, convert MORE SIGNAL into FAIL. Eternal “more research” is Chapter 2’s listening theater wearing a clipboard.

---

## What “Ugly Broom” Means in Product Language

Ugly does not mean careless ethics, dark patterns, or broken promises. Ugly means:

- Manual steps behind the curtain are allowed if the fire stops on schedule.
- UI can be plain; copy can be specific.
- Edge cases can be declined out loud (“we don’t do enterprise SSO in v0”).
- You solve the named fire, not the category fantasy.

Beautiful extinguishers are a later problem. Book 2 in *The Sea Playbook*—*Ship the Smallest Thing*—cares about observable delivery and getting paid. Here, beauty before fire-pass is how inspiration sneaks back wearing taste.

### Capability match (the quiet killer)

Sometimes the fire is real and you are the wrong firefighter. Maintenance too heavy, compliance too sharp, distribution owned by a platform that can deplatform you overnight, a niche that only buys through a channel you cannot enter. A FAIL can mean “not for us,” not “not real.” Write that distinction. It keeps the ledger honest and your ego less bruised. It also stops you from turning every real fire into a personal obligation.

### Overseas note (without turning this into a geo textbook)

Payment habits and “what counts as fire” shift by market. A workflow that is optional in one country can be deadline-shaped in another. When you build for overseas buyers, put country or community next to the scene on the fire page. Complaint signal that only exists in a language or forum you will never serve is interesting anthropology—not your broom.

---

## Gate Behavior in Meetings

When someone says “users are complaining, we should build,” answer with the page—not with charisma:

- Show the fire sentence.
- Show the grab-today substitute.
- Show the shape.
- Show three risks written first.
- Show PASS / FAIL / MORE SIGNAL.

If they only have vibes, you are not blocking innovation. You are blocking calendar arson.

Publicly log FAILs next to Chapter 2’s kills. A failed fire test is a win: you prevented polite spinning. If your culture turns FAILs into humiliation, shrink the audience until honesty is safe. The ritual matters more than the theater.

### The “we’ll make it must-have with marketing” trap

Marketing can amplify a fire. It rarely invents one. If your plan is “educate the market until they feel heat,” you are founding a school, not a broom shop. Schools can work. They are slower and capital-hungrier than most indie timelines allow. Say the strategy out loud if that is truly the bet—then admit you failed *this* gate on purpose, with eyes open. Silent failure of the gate while pretending you passed is how roadmaps become haunted.

### The “competitor has funding, so it must be must-have” trap

Funding is a bet. Bets are not fires. Well-funded nice-to-have dies politely in public all the time. Use competitors as sources of complaints and substitutes—not as proof that heat exists.

## Light Self-Falsification

**If everything PASSes,** your bar is vanity. Force a quota of FAILs for a month. Count them next to kills.

**If nothing PASSes,** you may be addicted to skepticism—or fishing in ponds with no wallets. Check whether “ugly broom acceptance” is set to impossible. Check whether you only read meme threads.

**If PASS always equals “build the platform,”** you are sneaking scope through the gate. PASS only unlocks a priced test or a tiny broom—not a rewrite of the industry.

**If you skip payment clues because “they’re suffering,”** remember: suffering without wallets is a charity brief. Charity can be noble. It is not the same as demand work.

**If you only fire-test indoors,** Steve Blank’s calendar constraint still applies: there are no facts inside your building, so get outside. Re-run with strangers’ words, not your Slack’s.

**If you treat the broom metaphor as permission to ship garbage ethics,** you misunderstood. Urgency does not waive clarity about price, data, and delivery.

### A five-minute drill you can run today

Pick one ledger theme marked `fire_test_next`. Set a timer for five minutes. Fill only questions 1–4 and the risk block on the fire page—no solution brainstorming allowed. When the timer ends, force a temporary label: likely PASS, likely FAIL, or MORE SIGNAL. Then schedule the honest version with a second source open in another tab. The drill trains speed without skipping evidence. Speed matters because teams use “we need a longer workshop” to avoid writing risks.

If the five-minute version already fails, trust it. Early FAIL is a gift. If it “feels like PASS,” do not celebrate—open the second source and try to kill it. Trying to kill a PASS candidate is how you earn the right to keep it.

## Back to the Shelf

The pretty extinguisher will keep calling. It photographs better. It fits “innovation” better. The broom looks like an insult to your craft.

Craft still matters—after the fire is real. Before that, craft is how teams decorate the wrong problem.

Chapter 3 gave you themes. Chapter 4 decides which themes deserve heat. Chapter 5 will force a job sentence so you do not pass a fire and then build a feature museum for a blurry hire.

Keep the broken broom in mind when the sticky notes start whispering again. The market does not owe your taste a standing ovation. It owes the fire a response.

## Field Notes From Rooms That Skipped the Gate

I have watched teams skip the fire test in three predictable costumes.

**Costume A — “We’ll learn by building.”**  
They mean they will learn by spending. Spending teaches, but it teaches expensively. A fire page costs an hour. A wrong microservice costs a quarter. If your learning plan requires endpoints before a grab-today substitute is named, you are not being empirical. You are being impatient with evidence that might hurt.

**Costume B — “Customers will tell us after launch.”**  
Sometimes they will. Often they will shrug, churn quietly, and leave you with a narrative about onboarding. Quiet shrugs are expensive because they arrive after the party photos. The fire test asks for heat *before* the party.

**Costume C — “Our vision includes educating the market.”**  
Vision can be real. It is still a different bet than must-have demand. Write “education bet” on the page if that is what you are doing. Then do not call it a PASS on Chapter 4. Mixing labels is how calendars get gaslit.

If you recognize your last project in a costume, you do not need punishment. You need a ritual: no ticket sizing until the fire page exists. Rituals beat New Year promises.

### Must-have adjacent: regulatory and deadline fires

Some fires are calendar-shaped because a regulator, a platform, or a contract deadline made them so. Those can be excellent must-haves—and terrible businesses if the deadline passes and never returns. Mark deadline fires with an expiry note. A broom sold for a one-time deadline can be a service sprint. It may not be a subscription story. Confused time shapes create confused products.


## Takeaway

**Fire test = must-have under pressure.** Hair on fire → broken broom beats pretty extinguisher.

**Four shapes:** high-frequency pain · scale disaster · dirty work · someone already pays.

**PASS unlocks priced validation or a tiny broom—not a six-month build.** FAIL or MORE SIGNAL means no serious development.

**Write ≥3 risks before the decision.** No risks on the page = no PASS.

**Template:** `templates/fire-test.md`.

**Watch for:** educating a market into feeling heat; “empty market” brags; borderline themes parked forever; funding mistaken for fire; beauty before proof.

# Chapter 5 — Jobs, Not Personas

Personas are flattering. Jobs are rude.

A persona lets a room agree on a cartoon: “Maya, 32, urban, power user, values simplicity.” Everyone nods. Nobody has to say what Maya is trying to finish before her train arrives. A job-to-be-done sentence is ruder. It names a situation, a struggle, and a progress the buyer wants. If you cannot write that sentence, you do not yet have demand standing on its feet—you have a mood board with demographics.

This chapter sits after the fire test on purpose. Heat without a job produces feature museums: rooms full of buttons that answer complaints one-by-one without ever being *hired* for a coherent piece of progress. Heat plus a clear job produces a broom with a handle—something a panicked hand can find in the smoke.

You will still meet people who say “we already know our user.” Sometimes they do. More often they know a persona slide. The test is simple: can they write the when / want / so-I-can line without glancing at the slide? If not, the slide was decoration.

---

## The Milkshake Logic (Public Attribution, Not Fan Fiction)

Clayton Christensen and colleagues popularized a hard teaching story in jobs-to-be-done work, retold in places such as *Competing Against Luck* and related Harvard Business School teaching materials: a fast-food chain wanted to improve milkshake sales. Early instincts pointed at the product—“make it tastier,” “thicken the profile,” optimize for a persona of milkshake lovers. Deeper attention to *when* and *why* people bought unsettled that frame.

In the morning, some buyers were hiring the milkshake for a long commute: thick enough to last, easy in one hand, more interesting than a boring breakfast. Later in the day, parents might hire a milkshake for a different job—something closer to a small, acceptable treat tied to time with a child. Same product category. Different jobs. Different improvement directions.

I am not quoting a private interview transcript. I am pointing at a publicly taught pattern you can verify in Christensen’s JTBD books and talks: **if you optimize “better milkshake” without naming the job, you improve the wrong attributes.** Morning-commute jobs care about duration, one-handed convenience, and surviving the drive. Evening-treat jobs care about portion, timing, and the social negotiation around “yes, you can have this.” Average those jobs into one “better shake” and you serve neither.

For indie builders, the milkshake is not about dairy. It is about refusing to let “users age 25–40 who like AI” replace “when I finish a client call on Thursday, I want to export markups that do not break, so I can send the file before dinner.”

### What to copy from the story (and what not to)

**Copy:** observe hiring moments; separate jobs that share a SKU; let the job choose which attributes matter; treat “do nothing” and “hire a human” as competitors.  
**Do not copy:** pretending you ran the original study; inventing dialogue; using “JTBD” as incense while still shipping feature bingo; forcing every product into a cute food metaphor in your investor update.

If a teammate starts a sentence with “According to the milkshake story…” and ends it with a feature list that ignores situations, interrupt kindly. The story is a warning about misplaced optimization—not a costume for the same old roadmap.

---

## Why Personas Fail Early Demand Work

Personas are not evil. They fail early demand work for mechanical reasons:

1. **They aggregate away the scene.** Averaging people deletes the Thursday handoff and the Monday export.
2. **They invite empathy theater.** Teams debate whether Maya would “vibe” with indigo or teal while the fire spreads in production.
3. **They smuggle inspiration.** Once Maya exists as a character, someone will invent what Maya “must want next.”
4. **They confuse identity with progress.** Buyers hire products for progress in a circumstance—not to join your persona club or your Notion aesthetic.
5. **They age badly across borders.** A US-coded persona paragraph translated into another market often keeps the adjectives and loses the job.

If you already have revenue and need marketing language, personas can return as communication tools. Before the gate, they are usually costumes for inspiration.

### The feature museum

Walk a museum: each room answers a complaint somebody once shouted. “Export CSV.” “Dark mode.” “AI summary.” “SSO someday.” No room answers *why today*. Visitors wander. Nobody hires the museum to finish a job before dinner. Complaint-led teams that skip JTBD recreate museums with better analytics—dashboards that prove you listened, not that you were hired.

A job sentence is a rope that ties features into a single hire—or proves half the features are souvenirs from a weekend brainstorm.

---

## The One-Liner You Will Actually Use

Write this until it hurts less:

> **When** [situation / trigger],  
> **I want to** [action / progress],  
> **so I can** [outcome that matters in that scene].

Examples (composites for teaching—not case-study promises):

- When I finish client markup every Thursday afternoon, I want to export a PDF that keeps comments and non-ASCII names, so I can send it before dinner without hiring a freelancer.
- When a new hire submits expenses on day four, I want to reject blurry receipts in one pass, so finance does not chase screenshots across Slack for a week.
- When my Shopify store hits ten SKUs of bundles, I want inventory math I do not trust a spreadsheet with, so a stockout does not surprise me on a paid-ad Monday.
- When I paste the same client brief into a chat tool every Monday, I want the constraints to persist, so I stop retyping the same guardrails before the real work starts.

Weak versions to reject on sight:

- When I am a busy professional, I want a better productivity suite, so I can be my best self. (Persona incense.)
- When I use software, I want AI, so I can save time. (Category fog.)
- When life is hard, I want delight, so I feel inspired. (Poster, not hire.)
- When our enterprise scales, I want digital transformation, so we achieve synergy. (Slide fungus.)

Template: `templates/jtbd-one-liner.md`.

### Stress tests for the sentence

1. **Independent rewrite:** Could two teammates independently write nearly the same sentence from the same ledger rows? If not, the job is still mush.
2. **Scene outcome:** Does the “so I can” name something that happens in a place and time? “So I can be efficient” fails. “So I can send before dinner” passes.
3. **Buzzword removal:** Strip AI / platform / seamless / delightful. Does progress remain intelligible? If not, the buzzword was doing the pretending.
4. **Competing hires:** Name what they grab instead—including “do nothing,” “ask an intern,” “pay a VA,” or “miss the deadline and apologize.” Jobs live in competitive sets. Lone jobs in a vacuum are usually fantasies.
5. **Ugly broom test:** Would they hire a plain solution that only completes this sentence? If they insist on a suite, you may have failed Chapter 4 while carrying Chapter 5 language.

## From Ledger Row to Job (A Small Worked Path)

Take Chapter 3’s Row B energy: Thursday PDF markups break; stack named; weekly schedule; paid partial fixes exist.

Fire test (Chapter 4): PASS on frequency + dirty work + already pays.

Now force the job without inventing a company:

- **Situation:** Thursday client handoff after markup.
- **Want:** export that preserves comments and names.
- **So I can:** send before dinner without a freelancer.

Notice what disappears: “AI-powered collaboration platform for modern agencies.” Notice what remains: a broom handle. Your `q3_wedge_opportunity` field in the ledger should now rhyme with this sentence. If the wedge says “rebuild document OS” while the job says “Thursday export,” you are already drifting into museum mode—usually because someone got excited about architecture.

Write competing hires beside the sentence: current PDF tool, freelancer on a marketplace, “send a folder of screenshots,” delay the client and absorb the shame. Pricing conversations later will argue with those hires. They will not argue with Maya’s favorite color.

### A second worked path (scale disaster)

Ledger theme: inventory for small Shopify sellers doing bundles. Already using spreadsheets. Fine at three SKUs; collapses near ten. Payment clue: people pay freelancers for “ops cleanup” after stockouts. Fire shape: scale disaster + already pays.

Job draft:

- When my bundle SKUs cross the edge of what a spreadsheet can hold in my head, I want trustworthy inventory math before I launch ads, so I do not sell air and refund in public.

Wedge rhyme: bundle-aware inventory check—not “full ERP.” Forbidden scope: warehouse robotics fantasies.

If your team keeps sliding into ERP language, re-read the sentence aloud until embarrassment does its job.

---

## Interviews Without Cosplay

You do not need a research department. You need fewer, sharper questions when you talk to humans—especially strangers, not friends who want you to succeed:

- Tell me about the last time this went wrong—where were you, what was the time pressure?
- What did you grab?
- What did you try that failed?
- What did it cost—hours, money, a client, sleep?
- What would you have paid *in that moment* to make it stop—not in abstract “value,” in cash or hours?
- If an ugly version only solved *that* moment, would you still use it?

Avoid leading with: “Would you use an AI-powered X?” Avoid touring your mockups first. Mockups teach people to perform politeness. Politeness is not a purchase order.

Capture their words into the ledger. Then rewrite *your* JTBD sentence until it can survive their words. If your sentence only survives your pitch deck, kill it without a eulogy.

### 5W2H as a scene restorer

When a job feels blurry, restore the scene with plain reporter questions: who, what, when, where, why, how, how much (time or money). You are not writing a novel. You are preventing the sentence from floating into brand fog. If “where” and “when” stay blank, you are still in persona land.

### Sampling without fake science

Five honest conversations with people who recently felt the fire beat fifty clicks on a survey that asks whether they “like innovation.” Surveys are not forbidden; they are weak as verdicts. Use them to find candidates for conversations, not to crown a job.

---

## Multiple Jobs, One Product (Choose a Battlefield)

Same buyer, multiple jobs—the milkshake pattern again. A freelancer might hire accounting software for “don’t get surprised by tax week” and separately for “look legit on an invoice.” A shop owner might hire a tool for inventory truth and also for “make me feel like a real operator.” If you optimize both at once with a tiny team, you will average yourself into mush.

Pick the battlefield job for v0. Write the other jobs in a parking lot labeled *not this broom*. Later books in *The Sea Playbook* can widen delivery. This book’s job is to stop you from pretending three battlefields are one PASS.

### Overseas and language

Jobs travel awkwardly across languages. A literal translation of your English one-liner may name a job nobody hires—or name a job that is already owned by a WeChat workflow you do not see from an English subreddit. When you build for overseas markets, rewrite the sentence with scene words from the communities where buyers already complain. Do not bless a translated persona paragraph and call it JTBD.

Country distribution belongs next to the situation clause when money will move across borders. A job that only exists where you cannot collect payment is a research note, not a product brief.

---



### Sticky-note jobs (a bad hybrid)

Some teams write JTBD on sticky notes the size of a fortune cookie: “When busy, want speed, so success.” That is persona incense with better grammar. If the note cannot hold a concrete Thursday, it is not a job sentence—it is a slogan. Use a full page. Jobs deserve the dignity of verbs and clocks.

## Gate Link: No Job, No Demand Standing

Add this checklist beside your fire decision:

- [ ] JTBD one-liner written in when / want / so-I-can form
- [ ] Competing hires listed (including do-nothing / human)
- [ ] Wedge rhymes with the job (not with a category reboot)
- [ ] At least one external quote or paraphrase supports the situation clause
- [ ] Buzzwords removed once as a stress test

If the fire PASSed but the job will not write, status becomes MORE SIGNAL or FAIL. Heat without hire direction is how teams fund museums and call it roadmap courage.

---

## Light Self-Falsification

**If every job sentence includes “AI,”** you are optimizing a hammer. Remove the word; see if progress remains.

**If jobs only appear inside your team,** you are interviewing a mirror. Mirrors flatter founders.

**If you collect twenty jobs and refuse to pick one,** you are running a museum with a bag check and calling it discovery.

**If “so I can” is always your metric vanity** (“increase engagement,” “drive retention”), you have swapped the buyer’s progress for yours.

**If you cite JTBD to end arguments without writing the sentence,** you are using theory as a club. Write the sentence or drop the acronym.

**If the sentence is beautiful and the ledger is empty,** you wrote poetry. Poetry is allowed in Seeds—not at the gate.

## Closing the Handle

Chapter 4 asked whether the fire is real. Chapter 5 asks what progress gets hired when it is. Together they stop two failure modes: building extinguishers for rooms without smoke, and building museums for smoke without a job.

Chapter 6 will insist that even a clean job still needs a chain of sources—pain, market behavior, competitor scars, and a technical window—before you trust it with a week you will not get back.

Until then, keep Maya off the whiteboard unless she can speak in when / want / so-I-can. Cartoons do not hire brooms. Situations do.

## Jobs Versus Outcomes Versus Metrics

Teams blur three layers:

- **Job:** progress in a situation (the hire).  
- **Outcome:** what “done” looks like in that scene (send before dinner).  
- **Metric:** what you measure internally (activation rate).

JTBD work lives in the first two. Metrics are servants. When metrics climb into the “so I can” clause—“so I can increase retention”—you have colonized the buyer’s sentence with your dashboard. Buyers do not wake up hiring you to move your charts. They wake up trying to get through Thursday.

Keep metrics on a separate sticky if you must. Never let them overwrite the job line.

### Switching costs as competing hires

Competing hires include inertia. “Do nothing” is often the strongest competitor because it is free and familiar. Your job sentence should make clear why doing nothing fails *this week*, not in abstract strategy time. If doing nothing is fine for another month, Chapter 4 probably should not have PASSed.


## Takeaway

**Buyers hire products for progress in a situation—not to join your persona.**

**Milkshake lesson (Christensen et al., public JTBD teaching / *Competing Against Luck* lineage):** same product, different jobs → different improvements. Do not average jobs into a mushy “better.”

**One-liner:** When… I want to… so I can…

**Stress:** scene outcome, competing hires, wedge rhyme, external words, buzzword removal.

**Template:** `templates/jtbd-one-liner.md`.

**Watch for:** feature museums; teal debates; translated personas; “AI” as a fake job; empty ledgers with pretty sentences.

# Chapter 6 — Four Sources, One Chain

A single screaming thread can ruin your month.

Not because the thread is false. Because it is lonely. Lonely signals feel vivid. Vivid is not the same as repeated, paid, or buildable. Teams that “move fast on insight” often move fast on weather. Weather is loud. Climate is quieter and crueler: it asks whether the same pain returns next week, whether wallets already move, whether incumbents are scarred in public, and whether you can deliver a broom without inventing a new branch of physics.

The cure is not “ignore communities.” The cure is a chain.

Handbook language worth keeping close: demand is often **stuck out, bought out, cursed out, and pushed out by technology**—not brainstormed into existence under fluorescent lights. Pain shows direction. Market behavior and competitor scars show that money and hatred already exist. A technical window shows you can ship something now. Skip a link and you will still feel busy. Busy is not the same as gated.

This chapter turns the chain into an opportunity card. Most cards should die. That is not pessimism. That is how you keep Chapter 4’s fire test from being undone by a charismatic screenshot.

---

## The Four Sources (As Lenses, Not Silos)

### 1. Real pain (direction)

Where people get stuck: your own snag if you are honest about sample-size-one, repeated complaints around you, community rants, “is there a tool for this?” posts, plugin one-stars, request boards, ticket tags if you already have users.

**You learn:** scenes, language, substitutes, emotional temperature, the words buyers already use for the fire.  
**You do not yet learn:** whether money will move toward *you*.

Pain is the compass. A compass is not a cash register. Compass-only teams build shrines to venting culture.

### 2. Market behavior (wallet weather)

Where money or measured intent already flows: freelance gigs with stable demand, rising or steady search interest, platform migrations that copy a job into a new surface, landing pages that already attract clicks, income boards that show someone getting paid for a related mess, pricing pages that exist because someone expected buyers.

**You learn:** the job is not only vented—it is funded or pursued somewhere.  
**You do not yet learn:** that your wedge will win.

Handbook reminder: keyword tools and trend charts are **sieves, not treasure basins**. An empty sieve can mean filters that are too harsh or a pond with no fish. It is not a moral verdict on your creativity. It is a measurement. Take another measurement before you invent a mythology.

### 3. Competitor scars (curse map)

Where incumbents earn one-stars and migration threads: review clusters, “switching from X” posts, feature-request graveyards, weak mobile edges, billing packs that force three-person teams into fifty-seat plans, export bugs that show up every Thursday in public.

**You learn:** the gap in concrete words.  
**You do not yet learn:** whether you can close it cheaper—or whether the scar is load-bearing because of sales motion, compliance, or a distribution lock you cannot see from a one-star.

“Shadow the weakness” is a useful phrase only if you can deliver the fix and survive how customers actually find you. Otherwise you are cosplaying strategy Twitter.

### 4. Technical window (now vs. never)

Where capability unlocked a migration: a model that makes a dirty middle step cheap enough, an API that removed a gate, an open-source core that only needs a thin opinionated shell, a payment or file format change that altered the job.

**You learn:** whether “we could build this” is timed to reality.  
**You do not yet learn:** whether anyone cares.

Technical windows without pain are how labs ship demos that collect compliments. Pain without windows is how essays stay essays. Both can be honest statuses. Neither is a PASS into heavy build.

---

## One Chain, Not Four Altars

Worshiping a single source creates signature diseases:

| If you only trust… | You tend to… |
| --- | --- |
| Pain communities | Build for venting culture; underweight payment; overfit to people who enjoy posting |
| Market / SEO charts | Chase volume words; miss whether a job exists; confuse navigation intent with hire intent |
| Competitor scars | Become a complaint mirror; ship bitterness; ignore why the incumbent still collects money |
| Tech windows | Ship demos looking for a hug; confuse capability with demand |

**Chain them in order you can explain to a tired teammate:**

1. Pain names a candidate job and scene (and feeds the ledger).  
2. Market behavior asks: is someone already paying, searching in loops, or hiring humans for this?  
3. Competitor scars ask: where does the current hire fail in public, in concrete language?  
4. Tech window asks: can we broom this now—and did we check open source before reinventing?

If a link is missing, write the hole on the card. Missing links are information. Pretending the chain is complete because you are excited is inspiration with a checklist costume.

### Cross-community rule

One platform on fire ≠ a trend. Cross-check across places buyers actually speak—threads plus reviews plus a search pass plus, when relevant, Discord chatter or ticket language. Single-platform emotion is a weather report. Weather reports are useful. They are not climate. Climate is what still hurts after the meme dies.

### Result-backward checks (optional fourth-and-a-half)

Sometimes you work backward from evidence that someone already captured attention: a competitor’s new sitemap URLs, landing pages with clicks, outbound traffic pointing at tools people recommend. These belong under market behavior. They do not replace pain. They stress-test whether your compass points somewhere wallets already wander.

## Worked Chain (Composite, Not a Promise)

**Pain:** Multiple threads and two plugin reviews describe Thursday markup export hell; non-ASCII names; weekly schedule; people describing twenty-minute workarounds.  
**Market:** Freelancers offering “PDF cleanup / client delivery ops” with repeat gigs; seats sold on partial SaaS fixes; search language that looks like job language, not pure brand navigation.  
**Scars:** One-stars about export; enterprise pricing packs; mobile markup abandoned; “we hired a VA for Thursdays.”  
**Tech window:** Libraries can parse/fix documents; productization is a workflow shell with an opinionated Thursday path—not a new PDF science project. Open-source check: core exists; shell + scene defaults do not.

Card status after fire PASS + JTBD: `ready_for_presale`. Not `build_document_os`. Forbidden scope stays visible so excitement cannot delete it overnight.

**Broken chain contrast:** a viral meme about “AI will replace PDFs,” a spike on a model-word, no payment fossils, scars that only say “feels meh,” a tech demo that impresses your group chat. Status: `killed` or `watch` with missing links named—not a two-month rewrite of PDF history.

### Search intent as a link (not a religion)

Chapter 1 mentioned a handbook case shape: roughly **eight** backlinks, keyword difficulty near **zero**, traffic near **~41K** monthly with a large search share on a brutally simple page. Study the shape: intent outside the building can look like evidence. Do not tattoo the numbers on your plan. Scene-words and model-words still die young. Long-term assets still need a product loop and a brand that survives the next hype wave. Put search in market behavior, then keep walking.

## Source Map You Can Actually Work

You do not need every channel. You need depth in a pond where your buyers already speak—and a second pond that can falsify the first.

**High-signal pain ponds (examples, not commandments):** vertical subreddits and help forums; plugin/store reviews with real usage and low stars; request boards where people write “I want…” in plain language; competitor Discords (listen; do not spam); your own support tags if you have them.

**A reusable four-question pass on a thread cluster** (sibling to Chapter 3): top concentrated complaints; substitutes named most; tool-shaped wedges; payment clues. AI can draft the pass. You still owe the kill decision.

**Market behavior ponds:** freelance marketplaces; Trends plus a second measuring stick; competitor top pages; landing-page click evidence when available; income/revenue boards for *structure*, not for envy cosplay.

**Scar ponds:** one-star clusters; migration threads; public roadmaps that never move; pricing pages that humiliate small teams; “workaround” blog posts that exist because the product failed a scene.

**Tech ponds:** GitHub search before manifestos; capability checks against the dirty middle step; API pricing that would not murder the wedge; honesty about maintenance.

**Automation boundary:** listen, clean, tag, draft summaries. Do not auto-post, auto-DM, or auto-argue in someone else’s community. Judgment stays human. A bot that turns ore into spam is how you get banned from the vein.

### Overseas practicality

For overseas products, put country distribution beside market behavior. A US-only scream may not fund a chargeback-prone bet in a market you do not understand. Payment stack reality belongs near the tech window too: a broom nobody can buy from you is a demo. Measuring sticks differ by region; imperfect measurement beats mystical confidence.

### Indie direction preference (chain hygiene)

Chains can be “correct” and still point at a bad life: endless tools for other indie hackers (inner loop), head-on war with a hundred-person team and a firehose of ads, or absolute-automation fantasies that erase human judgment. Prefer small traffic with clear willingness to pay over vanity traffic; ecological niches beside workflows people already run; dirty-work transfer; single-node systematization rather than “OS for the whole industry.” These are preferences from hard lessons, not commandments—and not revenue promises.

## Kill Criteria for Cards

Kill or demote when:

- Pain is vivid but unpaid and unsearched after a deliberate look.
- Market volume exists but intent is navigational or owned by a monopoly you cannot enter.
- Scars are aesthetic (“feels dated”) without a concrete, scene-tied gap.
- Tech window is “we like training models” without a Thursday.
- Open source already is the broom and your only edge is CSS.
- Risks are only the word “competition” with no sharper threat: compliance, support load, platform policy, distribution lock, maintenance gravity.
- The wedge cannot be said in one sentence without a platform fantasy.

Killed cards sit beside Chapter 2’s public kills. Patterns in kills teach. Hall-of-fame hopeful cards lie.

## Meeting Behavior

When someone drops a screenshot and says “this is huge,” ask for the card. If they cannot fill pain + at least one wallet/scar link + open-source honesty, you do not debate vibes. You schedule signal work—or you kill with a written reason.

If they fill the chain and the fire still PASSes, you still do not open the endpoint festival. You go to Chapter 7. Money is the next adult in the room.

## Light Self-Falsification

**If your cards always start from a model release,** you are doing tech theater. Start from pain at least half the time.

**If your cards never include open_source_gap,** you are volunteering to rebuild wheels for sport.

**If “market behavior” is only a vanity dashboard screenshot,** you are collecting posters.

**If you refuse to write risks first,** you will PASS everything that flatters you.

**If the chain is complete only inside your head,** write the URLs. Memory is not a source; memory is a storyteller.

**If every card becomes “watch” forever,** you built a museum of almost. Convert stale watches to kills on a timer.

## Thirty-Minute Chain Pass (When You Are Tempted to “Just Build”)

Set a timer. No Figma. No repo.

**Minutes 0–10:** Re-read two pain sources. Update ledger repeats. Rewrite the job one-liner once.  
**Minutes 10–18:** Find one market-behavior artifact (gig, pricing page, search note, landing evidence) or write `none yet` without flinching.  
**Minutes 18–24:** Capture one scar in concrete words—or `none yet`.  
**Minutes 24–28:** GitHub / open-source / capability note—or `not checked` (which blocks ready_for_presale).  
**Minutes 28–30:** Risks first (≥3), then status: watch / ready_for_presale / killed.

If you cannot finish in thirty minutes, the problem is usually missing sources—not missing talent. Go get sources. Do not “make time” by skipping risks.

This pass pairs with Chapter 9’s week. The week is a container. The thirty minutes is a unit you can run on an ordinary Tuesday when inspiration attacks.

### What “ready_for_presale” is allowed to mean

It means: you may spend a short budget of time and a small budget of money testing a priced promise. It does not mean: hire, rebrand, or announce a category. If your fingers itch to scaffold a monorepo the moment the card turns green, re-read forbidden_scope aloud. Itching is a symptom. Scope is the medicine.

## Bridge to Selling Air

A `ready_for_presale` card is a permission slip to test money—not to vanish for two months into architecture. Chapter 7 is the verdict chamber. Payment is a verdict. Likes are not. Pre-sales done cleanly are not a scam; silent shipping for months on untested heat often is a quieter way to waste a year.

Keep the chain visible when someone says “but Reddit loved it.” Love is a link. It is not the chain.

## Building a Tiny Opportunity Library (Without Becoming a Hoarder)

An opportunity library is a folder of cards with statuses and dates—not a graveyard of PDFs named `final_strategy`. Review it weekly the way you review the ledger: promote, kill, or demand a missing link. Cap active `watch` cards (for example, ten). When the eleventh arrives, kill one. Constraints create judgment.

Tag cards lightly: dirty-work, scale-disaster, search-shaped, service-to-software. Tags help you see if you are only hunting fashionable pain. Fashion is a single-source risk wearing brand.

### When market behavior conflicts with pain

Sometimes search volume is high and complaints are thin—or complaints are loud and wallets are absent. Do not average the conflict into a vibes PASS. Write the conflict as a risk. Then design a priced test that can kill one side of the story. Conflict is where learning lives. Pretending harmony is where roadmaps go to cosplay certainty.

# Interlude — GitHub Honesty & Search as a Demand Lens

Chapters 3–6 gave you ore, fire, jobs, and a chain. Two honesty moves still kill more fake projects than any brainstorm: **open-source comparison before you invent**, and **search intent used as a lens—not as a religion**.

This interlude sits between “I have a card” and “I sell air.” It is not Book 4 (SEO systems). It is the minimum measurement craft a demand worker needs so wallet weather and tech windows are not vibes.

---

## Part A — The GitHub / Open-Source Gate

### Why this gate exists

Builders love blank repos. Blank repos feel like progress. Many “unique” products are thin wrappers over libraries that already solve the dirty middle step—or full rewrites of tools users can already clone.

The handbook rule is blunt: **before you greenlight serious build (and before you treat a card as ready_for_presale), write what already exists in the open and why you are not just using it.**

Skipping the gate creates a signature failure: six weeks later you discover a maintained project that is 80% of your broom, and your only differentiation is CSS and a landing page you are emotionally attached to.

### What “checked” means (minimum bar)

A complete open-source honesty field answers:

1. **What exists?** Repo names, stars as a rough heat signal (not a quality verdict), last commit window, license shape at a glance.  
2. **What job does it already hire for?** Be fair. Do not strawman a library because the README is ugly.  
3. **What gap remains in *scene* terms?** Missing Thursday defaults, missing export path, missing opinionated workflow, missing non-ASCII handling—not “not delightful.”  
4. **Why won’t buyers just use it?** Learning cost, hosting pain, missing glue, trust, compliance, or true missing scene. If your answer is only “ours will be prettier,” you do not have a gap—you have taste.  
5. **Could your wedge be a thin shell?** Sometimes the honest product is configuration, templates, hosting, and support—not a ground-up engine.

If any of 1–4 is `not checked`, the card cannot say `ready_for_presale`. It can say `watch` or `killed`.

### Worked honesty blocks (composites)

**Block PASS-shaped (shell allowed):**  
“Libraries A/B parse and fill the document type. Last meaningful commits within months. License usable for a commercial shell. Gap: no opinionated weekly client-handoff workflow; non-ASCII names still break in the demos people actually share. Buyers currently hire VAs rather than self-host the libs. Wedge: hosted Thursday path + defaults—not a new PDF science project.”

**Block FAIL-shaped (reinvent theater):**  
“Project C already is a hosted broom for the same job; active; pricing below our planned number; our only listed edge is ‘AI-native redesign.’ Open-source core also exists with tutorials. Decision: kill self-build; optional note under Seeds for a niche template pack later—not a platform.”

**Block MORE SIGNAL:**  
“Two archived repos, one busy monorepo that might cover the job in a subdirectory we have not validated. Next: two hours of running the monorepo against our Thursday fixture file before any landing page.”

### Meeting language

When someone says “we should just build it, open source is messy,” answer:

“Messy is a maintenance estimate. It is not a free pass. Show the honesty block or we schedule the two hours—not the two months.”

When someone says “stars are low so it’s irrelevant,” answer:

“Stars measure attention, not fit. Run our fixture. Fit is a file, not a vibe.”

### GitHub gate checklist (paste under tech_window)

```text
[ ] Searched for the job words + format words + “export/CLI/API”
[ ] Ran at least one existing tool/lib against a real fixture (or timed why not)
[ ] Wrote gap in scene language
[ ] Wrote why-not-just-use-it without “prettier”
[ ] Named shell-vs-engine decision
[ ] License / hosting / maintenance note (even one line)
[ ] If reinventing engine: explicit reason + risk line for reinvent cost
```

Fail any of the first four → no `ready_for_presale`.

### Boundary

This gate is not anti-build. It is anti-amnesia. Plenty of businesses are opinionated shells. Shells can be honest, priced, and useful. Engines can be honest too—when the gap is real and the maintenance is priced into your life.

## Part B — Search Intent as a Demand Lens (Not an SEO Book)

### What this part is for

Search behavior is one way strangers outside your building reveal jobs. Used well, it feeds **market behavior** on the opportunity card. Used badly, it becomes keyword cosplay: volume boards, KD fantasies, and a site nobody hired.

Book 4 will go deep on systems. Here you only need lenses that prevent two errors:

1. Ignoring measured intent that already looks like a job.  
2. Mistaking navigational or monopoly-owned queries for a product invitation.

### Three lenses (word → site → site)

Keep the handbook trio tiny:

| Lens | Question | Demand use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Word → site** | Who already ranks or sells around this job language? | See if the job is already a business surface |
| **Site → word** | What language does a paid/traffic site actually eat? | Steal vocabulary for ledger and page, not for spam |
| **Site → site** | What else looks like the same commercial cluster? | Spot whether the pond is a one-off or a copyable mess |

You are not building a content factory tonight. You are checking whether wallet weather exists.

### Job language vs navigation language

**Job-shaped queries** often include a scene or outcome: export, fix, without, for client, weekly, alternative to, how do I when…  
**Navigation-shaped queries** often want a known brand or login: brand name alone, “official,” “download [brand],” portal names.

Navigation heat can be enormous and still worthless as a first indie wedge—especially when a platform owns the intent. If your “opportunity” is ranking for someone else’s front door, you are not finding demand; you are auditioning for a lawsuit or a treadmill.

### Sieves, not treasure basins

Keyword tools and trend charts are **sieves**. An empty sieve can mean harsh filters or no fish. It is not a moral verdict on your creativity.

Practical micro-routine (30–40 minutes max when a theme is a candidate):

1. Write the job one-liner.  
2. List 5–10 phrases buyers already used in complaints (steal from the ledger).  
3. Check whether those phrases (or close cousins) show up as recurring search language or as pages that already attract clicks.  
4. Note country distribution if you are shipping overseas—imperfect sticks beat mystical confidence.  
5. Write on the card: `market_behavior: …` or `none yet`.  
6. **Stop.** Do not open twenty tools to postpone the fire test.

### Teaching-case shape (not a target)

Chapter 1 mentioned a handbook case shape: a brutally simple page, roughly **eight** backlinks, keyword difficulty near **zero**, traffic near **~41K** monthly with a large search share. Read it as: **outside intent can look like evidence when the page matches a real ask.**

Do not read it as: copy thin pages and inherit a living. Scene-words and model-words die young. Long-term assets still need a product loop and a brand that survives the next hype wave. Case numbers are not OKRs.

### Result-backward peeks (optional)

When available without turning into a research career:

- New landing URLs on a competitor’s site (what they are trying to catch)  
- Outbound traffic toward tools people recommend  
- Income / revenue boards for **structure** (who gets paid for which mess)—not for envy cosplay  

These belong under market behavior. They never replace pain sources.

### Red lines for this interlude

1. **No SEO roadmap inside a complaint sprint.** If you are writing a three-month content calendar before a priced ask, you left Book 1.  
2. **No KD worship.** Difficulty scores are inputs to humility, not trophies.  
3. **No invented “search volume proves they’ll pay.”** Search proves curiosity or navigation more often than checkout. Payment still needs Chapter 7.  
4. **No skipping GitHub because “SEO will save us.”** Distribution does not excuse reinventing a broom that already ships.  
5. **No typo / grey / monopoly-front-door games as a first bet** if you cannot explain compliance and maintenance like an adult. Beginners: stay in boring job language.

### Tie-back to the chain

```text
Pain (ledger) 
   → market behavior (including search lenses + service fossils)
   → competitor scars
   → tech window + GitHub honesty
   → priced air
```

If search is the only bright cell on the card, you have a traffic hypothesis looking for a job. Flip back to complaints before you fall in love with a keyword.

## Part B2 — Overseas Measuring Sticks (Without Turning Into Ads/SEO)

If you build for buyers outside one home market, “the search chart said so” is incomplete. Demand work still starts with complaints. Measurement sticks decide whether wallet weather is *reachable* for you.

### Google as radar, Bing as a ruler (teaching metaphor)

A useful handbook contrast: one engine often feels like a **radar** for what is bubbling; another’s keyword research views can feel like a **ruler** for magnitude, country mix, and who already occupies the first page. Neither replaces a ledger. Both can falsify a fantasy that “everyone” wants your broom.

When a theme survives fire on English complaints, spend a short block asking:

1. **Where are the speakers?** Country distribution matters for payment rails, chargebacks, support hours, and whether your price is polite or absurd.  
2. **Is the intent job-shaped in that country mix?** Or is it branded navigation you cannot own?  
3. **Who occupies the first results?** Agencies, incumbents, official docs, spam thin pages—each implies a different wedge (or a walk-away).  
4. **Did the same complaint show up in the communities of that country/language?** Single-country scream ≠ global TAM bedtime story.

Write answers under `market_behavior` in plain words. If you cannot measure cleanly, write `measurement incomplete` and raise a risk—do not invent a global market from a US meme.

### Trends and timing humility

Trend tools lag. Model-words and scene-words spike and die. If your card depends on a spike younger than your sprint, label it short-line and demand a priced ask fast—or kill when the spike is the only heat. Long-term wedges prefer job language that still makes sense when the hype thread is forgotten.

### What not to do with measuring sticks

- Do not open Ads Manager to “validate demand” before a Complaint Ledger theme exists. That is Book 3 energy wearing a lab coat.  
- Do not build a content calendar from KD tables during Days 1–5.  
- Do not treat CPC as proof of willingness to pay *you*—only as weather about auctions.  
- Do not skip GitHub because “traffic will be large.” Large traffic on a reinvented wheel is a louder way to waste a year.

### Mini worked note (composite)

```text
market_behavior: EN forum pain repeats; freelance cleanup gigs exist; 
search language looks job-shaped ("weekly client markup export") not brand-only;
country peek: majority EN-US/UK speaking surfaces we can support in our hours;
ruler note: first page occupied by partial SaaS + tutorial blogs — room for opinionated Thursday shell, not for "new PDF platform";
risk: we have not checked non-EN ponds yet — overseas expansion is a later hypothesis, not a PASS claim.
```

That paragraph is what honesty looks like. It is dull. Dull ships.

# Chapter 7 — Sell Air First

“Sell air” sounds like a scam. That is why careful people flinch—and then quietly waste a year building oxygen tanks for a planet with no buyers.

In this book, selling air means something narrower and cleaner: **a clear promise, a real price, and a way for a stranger to pay or firmly commit before you build the heavy version.** You say what you will do, what fire you stop, how you will stop it, and what it costs. You do not take money for a fantasy you have no intention of delivering. You do not hide behind “beta vibes” to avoid naming a number. You also do not treat likes, waitlist emails, or friend cheers as a verdict.

Payment is a verdict. Applause is weather.

Early priority, said without romance: **demand before traffic theater before product polish.** More precisely for this chapter: after a fire PASS, a job sentence, and a chain that is not a single meme—**test money before you test your willingness to live in a codebase for a quarter.**

If that sentence feels rude, good. Rudeness here is a courtesy to your future self.

---

## What Selling Air Is (and Is Not)

**Is:**

- A landing page, sales note, or call script that states the job, the broom, the limitations, and a price.
- A Stripe link, checkout, invoice, or deposit that a stranger can complete without joining your friendship circle.
- A manual delivery path (“we do first customers by hand”) stated up front, not confessed later as an apology.
- A short window: days, not “someday when the platform is ready.”
- A willingness to refund or stop cleanly if you cannot deliver what you promised.

**Is not:**

- Fake scarcity countdown theater with no product intent.
- Vague “join the revolution” pages with no price and no job.
- Harvesting emails for a waitlist so you can feel popular in a standup.
- Charging for vapor you already know you will not ship.
- Using friends-and-family courtesy purchases as proof of market.
- “Soft interest” spreadsheets that never meet a checkout.

Ethics are not a side essay. They are load-bearing. The whole point of a verdict is that it is honest. A dishonest pre-sale teaches you the wrong lesson twice: you learn nothing about demand, and you train yourself to treat buyers as props for your inspiration play.

### Why keep the uncomfortable phrase

“Air” reminds you how little of the product must exist for a verdict. A form, a promise, a payment link, and a calendar can be enough. Teams that insist “we can’t ask for money until it’s perfect” are often protecting ego, not buyers. Buyers can handle an ugly broom. They cannot handle being used as unpaid audience. Perfection-first is how nice-to-have rebuilds the extinguisher factory while the ledger gathers dust.

Same-structure cousin from consumer commerce: confirm demand, then produce. The surface differs. The order rhymes.

---

## Likes Are Not Payment (A Taxonomy of Weak Signals)

Rank signals like an adult:

1. **Money moved** (strongest early verdict).  
2. **Strong commitment with friction** (deposit, signed pilot, scheduled paid onboarding, invoice accepted).  
3. **Priced intent** (clicked checkout, then abandoned—informative, not victory).  
4. **Unpriced intent** (waitlist email, “notify me”).  
5. **Social warmth** (likes, “so cool,” friend DMs, award emojis).  

Most teams overweight 4 and 5 because they are easy to collect and easy to celebrate. This chapter forces you to design for 1 and 2. If your dashboard only shows 4 and 5, you do not have validation—you have a fan club newsletter.

Friend money is a special hazard. Price a little higher than feels comfortable if your only buyers so far are people who love you. Courtesy purchases are hugs with receipts. Hugs are good. They are not climate. Raise the number until hugs hesitate. Hesitation is information.

### Surveys and “would you pay?” interviews

Asking “would you pay?” is not the same as presenting a price and a button. Hypothetical wallets are generous. Real checkouts are not. Use conversations to sharpen the job (Chapter 5). Use checkout to host the verdict (this chapter). Do not let a survey average impersonate a payment.

---

## The Seven-Day Verification Script

This is a script, not a guarantee. Handbook figures elsewhere in the series remain teaching cases, not promises you inherit by running a template.

### Day 0 prerequisites (do not skip)

- Fire test PASS—or an explicit “testing to kill” note (rare; be honest if that is the posture).  
- JTBD one-liner that survives buzzword removal.  
- Opportunity card with ≥3 risks written first.  
- Open-source gap checked (not “we’ll be cleaner”).  
- Forbidden scope written where you can see it while drafting copy.  
- A delivery story you could tell without blushing (even if manual).

If Day 0 is incomplete, you are not in a seven-day script. You are in a costume.

### Days 1–2 — Promise on a page

Write problem-first copy:

- Who is on fire (scene, not persona soup).  
- What they grab today.  
- What your broom does—and does not do yet.  
- Price (real number; currency clear).  
- How delivery works in v0 (manual OK).  
- How to pay / how to talk to a human.  
- Refund or stop rules in plain language.

Avoid: feature museums, brand poetry, “AI-powered” as a substitute for a job, fake logos, MRR ladders, screenshots of vanity analytics. If a sentence could advertise anything, delete it. If a sentence promises income outcomes, delete it twice.

A simple page beats a clever one. Clever pages are how builders delay the verdict while congratulating themselves on craft.

### Days 2–3 — Payment path live

Put a real checkout or invoice path in front of strangers. If your ideal stack is not ready, use boring tools. Fancy billing taxonomies can wait for Book 2. The verdict cannot wait for elegance.

Test the path yourself on a different browser as if you were slightly hostile. Broken checkouts create false “no demand” stories.

### Days 3–7 — One or two traffic ponds, not twelve

Take the page where your pain sources already live—or adjacent ponds where the same job speakers gather. Depth beats spray. Helpful participation with clear disclosure is different from spam. If a community bans promotion entirely, do not launder spam as “research.” Pick a pond that allows honest offers, or use a small paid-traffic cap knowing **ads buy time, not PMF** (Book 3’s argument). For this book, ads are optional salt—not the meal.

Measure roughly without building a BI religion:

- Visits that look like the intended audience.  
- Checkout starts.  
- Payments or firm commitments.  
- Questions people ask (often richer ore than your headline).  
- Refund requests and why (early truth).

### End of day 7 — Verdict meeting (thirty minutes)

No slide deck required. Risks first, again. Then choose in writing:

- **Money moved / strong commits:** proceed toward the smallest shippable broom (Book 2), still inside forbidden_scope. Schedule delivery dates you can keep.  
- **Interest without money:** revise promise, price, pond, or job—or kill. Do not “just build a little to help the marketing.”  
- **Silence:** treat as a gift. Silence is cheaper than a quiet launch after three months of endpoints. Kill or return to signal work with a new card—not with a secret rewrite.

Pre-sale with no buyers is not humiliation. It is a calendar refund. Put it next to public kills so the culture learns to value it.

```text
- What we do
- What we refuse to do yet
- Manual steps you should expect

Price
- Number + what it includes
- Refund / stop rules

Proof posture
- Constraints you accept; scars you studied; no fake logos

CTA: Pay / Book paid setup / Request invoice
```

Tone matches this series: perimeter visible, no guaranteed revenue tier, no entrepreneurial cosplay. If you need hype to make the page convert in your imagination, the job may not be standing in reality.

## Manual Delivery Is Allowed (And Often Wise)

First customers can be handcrafted. That is not a betrayal of SaaS identity. That is how you learn the job’s sharp edges before you harden them into code. Say it on the page. People who require a fantasy of pure automation on day one will filter themselves out—often a blessing.

Absolute automation as a day-one must is a recurring false demand. Automation amplifies a process. If the process is wrong, automation amplifies waste. Sell the stopped fire first. Automate after the verdict and after you have watched humans stumble through the scene.

### Capacity honesty

If ten people paid tomorrow, could you deliver without lying? If not, lower the marketing surface, raise the price, or narrow the cohort. Selling air does not mean selling infinite air. It means selling a promise you can carry.

## Worked Mini-Example (Composite, Not a Forecast)

Card: Thursday markup export broom. Price: high enough that friends flinch. Page promises: share the broken path; receive a fixed file within N hours for the first cohort; automation only after repeated paid weeks. Traffic: two communities where the threads already existed + optional small search spend with a hard cap.  

- **Outcome A:** a few strangers pay—learn delivery, shrink scope to the repeated step, write forbidden_scope bigger.  
- **Outcome B:** many “interested” DMs, zero checkouts—reposition or kill; do not start a document OS.  
- **Outcome C:** silence—kill with gratitude; return to the chain.

None of these outcomes promises a revenue tier. All beat polite spinning. Handbook repair stories at roughly **$10k MRR** scale remain someone else’s scoreboard—useful as shape, toxic as prophecy.

## Objections You Will Hear (And Straight Answers)

**“It’s unethical.”**  
Lying is unethical. Clear promise + price + delivery path is a contract conversation. If you cannot describe delivery, you are not ready to charge—and not ready to build much either.

**“Nobody will pay before it’s polished.”**  
Some will not. That is data. Others already pay VAs and ugly tools. If only polish-hungry buyers appear, you may still be in nice-to-have land (return to Chapter 4).

**“We’ll lose the surprise launch.”**  
Surprise launches are theater. Verdicts are quieter and more useful. Theater can come later if you still care.

**“Let’s build a huge waitlist first.”**  
Waitlists without price train compliment collection. Add a number—even if the button says “reserve with deposit.”

**“Our enterprise cycle needs six months.”**  
Then your “air” may be a paid pilot SOW, not a low checkout. The shape changes; the verdict principle does not. Do not use “enterprise” as a permanent shield against asking.

**“We’ll validate with ads after the product is nice.”**  
That swaps the order this series defends. Ads can buy learning; they cannot invent a job that failed a priced page.

---



## Pricing Without Prophecy

Price is part of the verdict apparatus, not a separate “growth chapter” you postpone. Too low and you buy hugs. Too high without delivery clarity and you buy silence that you misread as “no demand for the job” when it may be “no trust for the promise.”

Practical stance for Book 1:

- Start from substitutes: what do VAs, freelancers, or ugly SaaS seats cost for the scene?  
- Place your number in argument with those hires—not in argument with your fear.  
- Prefer a clean monthly or one-time number over a labyrinth of tiers in week one.  
- Write what happens if they pay and you under-deliver.  
- Do not paste handbook revenue cases into your price logic.

If you cannot name substitutes’ costs, you are not ready to price—or ready to claim already_pays as a fire shape. Go back to the chain.

### Refunds as truth serum

Refunds sting. They also teach. A pre-sale that forbids refunds “to increase commitment” can hide a muddy promise. Clarity beats trickery. If refunds cluster around the same misunderstanding, your page lied by accident—fix the words before you blame the market.

## Gate: What This Chapter Unlocks

Selling air unlocks learning and, sometimes, early cash. It does not unlock a rewrite of your identity into “growth hacker.” It does not replace Book 2’s craft of shipping the smallest observable thing and collecting payment without myths. It sits between opportunity and build as a bouncer with a receipt printer.

If money moves, resist scope inflation. Buyers hired a broom. Do not surprise them with a museum and call it delight. If money does not move, resist the story that “marketing was wrong” until promise, price, and pond have been honestly tested. Sometimes marketing is wrong. Often the job was never standing.

## Scripts for Asking Without Cringing

You can say:

- “We’re offering a manual first version at this price to people who have the Thursday problem. If it’s not your problem, please ignore.”  
- “Here’s exactly what we won’t do yet. If you need those, we’re the wrong broom.”  
- “If nobody buys this week, we’ll kill it. That’s the point.”

Cringe often comes from overclaiming. Under-claim cleanly and the ask gets easier. If you still cringe at a clear promise, examine whether you believe the fire. Disbelief is useful data—return to Chapters 4–6 before you push traffic.

### International payments footnote

Overseas charging adds friction: currency, tax display, chargebacks, local methods. Friction is not an excuse to skip price. It is a reason to state what you support and what you do not. A narrow, honest checkout beats a fake “global” button that fails at the last step and pollutes your verdict.

# Chapter 8 — Where Demand Finding Breaks

Every useful method dies in characteristic ways. If you do not name those ways, the method becomes a costume for the same old inspiration—only now with spreadsheets.

This chapter is self-falsification on purpose. Complaint ledgers, fire tests, job sentences, four-source chains, and pre-sales can each rot. Rotten tools still look like tools. That is why they are dangerous.

Read this as red lines: conditions under which you should **stop using this method as currently practiced**—not as poetic humility at the end of a blog post.

---

## Failure Mode 1 — Single-Source Emotion

**What it looks like:** one subreddit mood, one viral dunk, one Discord meltdown, one influencer rant. The ledger fills with screenshots from a single weather system.

**Why it breaks:** emotion is contagious; contagion is not frequency; frequency is not payment. You will overfit to people who enjoy posting.

**Brake:** require independent repeats across time or platform before `theme` becomes `fire_test_next`. If you cannot find a second pond, write `MORE SIGNAL`—or kill.

**Stop using the method when:** you catch yourself saying “the internet is angry about X” as if that were a card.

---

## Failure Mode 2 — Complaint → Feature Bingo

**What it looks like:** every insult becomes a ticket. Roadmaps become museums (Chapter 5). “We listen” becomes a synonym for “we cannot choose.”

**Why it breaks:** complaints are inputs to jobs and fires, not a shopping list. Bingo creates products with no hireable progress.

**Brake:** no feature earns a ticket until it rhymes with a JTBD one-liner and a PASS fire page. Park extras in a souvenir lot.

**Stop using the method when:** your “complaint-driven roadmap” is longer than your forbidden_scope list.

---

## Failure Mode 3 — Skipping Open-Source (and Other Honesty Fields)

**What it looks like:** `open_source_gap: not checked` for weeks; “ours will be cleaner”; rebuilding libraries with better CSS; ignoring a marketplace that already sells the broom as a service.

**Why it breaks:** you spend your scarce months on undifferentiated rebuilds. Demand may be real and still not worth *your* build.

**Brake:** no `ready_for_presale` while open-source (or “buy the service”) is unchecked. Write why you still deserve to exist in one uncomfortable paragraph.

**Stop using the method when:** honesty fields become optional decorations.

---

## Failure Mode 4 — Eternal Another Week of Digging

**What it looks like:** MORE SIGNAL forever; research cosplay; dashboards of pain; no kills; no priced page.

**Why it breaks:** listening theater (Chapter 2) returns wearing a clipboard. You feel rigorous while avoiding verdicts.

**Brake:** timers. Two weeks max on MORE SIGNAL without either a kill, a PASS+presale, or a written reason to extend once. Count kills as output.

**Stop using the method when:** “we’re still validating” is your personality.

---

## Failure Mode 5 — Automation Past the Boundary

**What it looks like:** auto-commenting, auto-DMing, auto-posting “helpful” prompts into communities; bots that turn ore veins into spam; scrapers that violate terms you pretend not to see.

**Why it breaks:** you poison the well, get banned, and teach your team that demand work equals acquisition abuse. Also: automated judgment is usually automated bias.

**Brake:** automation may listen, clean, tag, and draft. Humans send, judge, and kill. Period.

**Stop using the method when:** your “research stack” requires hiding your identity from the communities you mine.

---

## Failure Mode 6 — Fire Test as Vibes

**What it looks like:** PASS without risks; PASS without a grab-today substitute; everything is must-have because you like it; marketing will “create the fire.”

**Why it breaks:** Chapter 4 becomes a rubber stamp. Nice-to-have wears a broom costume.

**Brake:** risks first; ugly-broom acceptance question mandatory; quota of FAILs.

**Stop using the method when:** PASS rate approaches 100%.

---

## Failure Mode 7 — JTBD as Incense

**What it looks like:** acronyms without sentences; sticky-note slogans; “AI” in every want-clause; personas renamed as jobs.

**Why it breaks:** you get the prestige of theory without the rudeness of specificity.

**Brake:** when / want / so I can on a full page; buzzword removal stress test; competing hires listed.

**Stop using the method when:** nobody can point to the current battlefield job without opening a slide.

---

## Failure Mode 8 — Pre-Sales as Theater or Slime

**What it looks like:** waitlists without prices; fake countdown ethics; friend-only receipts; charging for what you cannot describe; extending “seven days” into a quarter to avoid writing a kill.

**Why it breaks:** you either learn nothing or learn how to disrespect buyers. Both destroy the verdict.

**Brake:** real number, stranger path, delivery story, refund clarity, hard stop date.

**Stop using the method when:** you need hype language to feel OK about the page.

---

## Failure Mode 9 — Inner-Loop Products and Wrong Competitors

**What it looks like:** endless tools for other indie hackers; hard wars against hundred-person teams with ad firehoses; “OS for everything” wedges; boring industry dirty work ignored because it is unfashionable.

**Why it breaks:** the chain can look correct while your life becomes impossible. Method success ≠ life fit.

**Brake:** prefer niches beside real workflows with wallets; write competitor realism in risks; allow “real but not for us” FAILs.

**Stop using the method when:** every card targets people exactly like your Twitter mutuals.

---

## Failure Mode 10 — Turning the Perimeter into Fine Print

**What it looks like:** handbook cases (~**$10k MRR** repair stories, ~**8** backlinks / ~**41K** visits shapes) quietly become OKRs; “no promises” in the preface, prophecy in the standup.

**Why it breaks:** you reintroduce inspiration as numeric destiny. The method’s honesty dies first; the calendar dies second.

**Brake:** say “case, not promise” out loud when numbers appear. Ban copying case numbers into goals.

**Stop using the method when:** you catch yourself selling certainty to yourself.

---

## Five Red Lines (Print Them)

1. **No single-source PASS.**  
2. **No feature tickets without a job rhyme.**  
3. **No ready_for_presale with open-source unchecked.**  
4. **No automation that speaks to communities as if it were you without you.**  
5. **No seven-day script without a dated verdict—including kill.**  

If you break a red line, you do not need a workshop. You need a stop. Repair the practice, then continue. Continuing while broken is how methodologies become cult kitsch.


Postmortems without blame theater beat silent disappearances. Silent disappearances teach nothing and keep the haunted roadmap alive.

## What Still Works When Parts Break

Even if you temporarily stop “the full method,” you can keep smaller hygiene: write risks first; prefer stranger words; treat payment as stronger than applause; kill in public. Hygiene is the portable core. Frameworks are scaffolding. Do not burn the core because a scaffold warped.

This chapter can itself become performative—listing failure modes as virtue without changing behavior. If you highlight these lines and still run feature bingo next week, you did not read; you decorated.

Pick one failure mode you personally commit. Put it on a sticky where you cannot help seeing it. That sticky is allowed. Inspiration stickies still are not.

## How Rot Shows Up in Standups

Listen for these phrases. They are smoke alarms:

- “Everyone’s talking about it.” (single-source / weather)  
- “We’ll just add it to the backlog.” (feature bingo)  
- “Open source isn’t polished enough.” (unchecked honesty wearing taste)  
- “Give us one more week of research.” (eternal digging)  
- “The bot can engage for us.” (automation boundary breach)  
- “It’s obviously must-have.” (vibe PASS)  
- “Our JTBD is innovation.” (incense)  
- “The waitlist is exploding.” (unpriced theater)  
- “Our users are builders like us.” (inner loop)  
- “This could be a $10k MRR thing like that case.” (perimeter collapse)

When you hear one, you do not need a moral speech. Ask for the artifact: second source, job sentence, open-source note, kill date, checkout link, risk block. Artifacts beat arguments.

### Team size note

Solo builders rot too—usually via eternal digging and vibe PASSes, because nobody is there to be rude. If you are solo, schedule a weekly “stranger review”: send one fire page or one landing draft to someone who does not share your upside. Pay them in cash or favor. Friendship-only review recreates friend-money bias.

## Recovery Plan After You Catch Yourself

1. **Stop the build** that depended on the rotten step.  
2. **Write the failure mode number** in the card/ledger.  
3. **Restore the missing artifact** or kill.  
4. **Add one process scar:** a checklist item, a timer, a red line on the wall.  
5. **Only then** resume.

Skipping to step 5 is how smart teams stay stuck. Shame is optional. Sequence is not.

## When the Market Moves Faster Than Your Ledger

Sometimes a real window is short: a platform change, a policy shift, a model capability. Speed matters—but speed without red lines is just panic with better tools. Compress the chain into the thirty-minute pass (Chapter 6), run a shorter priced test, and accept that some windows close. Closed windows are not personal insults. They are weather. Do not use “speed” as a reason to delete open-source checks or refund clarity.

Methods should make you faster at killing and clearer at shipping verdicts—not holier.

## A Quarterly Method Audit (Fifteen Minutes)

Once a quarter, skim your last ten cards and ask:

- How many single-source PASSes slipped through?  
- How many features shipped without job rhyme?  
- How often was open-source “not checked”?  
- How many MORE SIGNALs aged past two weeks?  
- Did any bot speak for us?  
- What was our FAIL rate on fire tests?  
- Did any case number become a goal?

Score lightly. Pick one scar to fix. Do not create a bureaucracy that replaces shipping verdicts. The audit exists to keep Chapter 8 from becoming unread scripture.




## Case Study Shape: A Method That Ate Itself (Composite)

A small team ran ledgers faithfully for a month. Row count looked impressive. They declared themselves “complaint-driven.” Then the rot arrived in a bundle: they auto-drafted replies in a forum; they PASSed three fires in one afternoon without risks; they shipped a changelog of bingo features; they cited a handbook-like revenue case in a Friday all-hands as if it were a forecast. Two months later they had a quieter chart and a louder story about “market timing.”

What would have saved them was not more ideology. It was red lines with teeth: no bot speech; one PASS per week maximum until FAILs appeared; feature freeze until job rhyme; ban on pasting case numbers into goals; a dated pre-sale that could kill the favorite theme. The method did not fail as math. It failed as discipline.

If this composite feels uncomfortably close, treat that feeling as a gift. Closeness means you can intervene before the quiet chart.

### The “we’re different” exemption

Everyone’s favorite escape hatch is uniqueness: our market is different; our founders are technical enough to skip air; our community hates selling; our enterprise buyers never pay early. Sometimes constraints are real. Write them as risks and redesign the verdict surface (paid pilot, SOW, deposit). Do not write them as a blanket exemption from Chapter 8. Uniqueness is usually ego’s preferred citation format.

## Teaching Juniors Without Creating Zealots

If you bring juniors into complaint work, teach artifacts before vocabulary. A filled fire page beats a lecture on Christensen. A kill sentence beats a quiz on Blank. Zealots memorize words and bully teammates with acronyms. Practitioners produce dated decisions.

Pair juniors with a red-line buddy for one sprint. The buddy’s job is not to be clever. It is to point at missing fields. Missing fields are how juniors learn; humiliation is how they learn to hide.

# Chapter 9 — One-Week Complaint Sprint

You now have more framework than a week can worship. Good. This chapter is a container, not a new religion: **seven days to move from empty air to a verdict**, using Chapters 3–8 as brakes and tools.

If you try to do everything perfectly, you will invent Failure Mode 4 (eternal digging) inside a calendar that looked like a sprint. The sprint’s job is to force artifacts and a dated decision—not to make you holy.

At the end, a short bridge points to Book 2 of *The Sea Playbook*: *Ship the Smallest Thing*. Do not sneak-build Book 2 during Day 3 because coding feels safer than asking for money.

---

## Sprint Rules (Pin These)

1. **One vertical pond** as primary; one secondary for cross-check. Not twelve.  
2. **Risks before conclusions** any time you make a gate call.  
3. **Public kills** (even if “public” means your tiny team doc).  
4. **No serious feature build**—prototypes that serve the priced page are allowed; platforms are not.  
5. **Red lines from Chapter 8 stay on.**  
6. **Case numbers are not goals.**  
7. **End of Day 7 has a written verdict:** kill / revise-and-reschedule / proceed to smallest ship.

Miss a day? Compress. Do not silently extend into a lifestyle.

---

## Day 1 — Choose the Pond and Open the Ledger

**Morning:** Pick a vertical where buyers already talk: a subreddit, review surface, plugin store niche, request board, or industry forum. Write why these speakers can pay (even roughly). If you cannot, pick another pond.

**Afternoon:** Create the Complaint Ledger file (`templates/complaint-ledger.md`). Add a Seeds column for inspiration so it does not contaminate evidence. Capture 10–20 raw artifacts minimum—titles, URLs, ugly quotes. Do not solutioneer.

**Output:** pond named · ledger opened · first rows with sources.

**Fail check:** if you spend Day 1 designing a logo, you have left the sprint.

---

## Days 2–3 — Structure, Dedupe, Four Questions

Run Chapter 3 hard.

- Fill three-part signal fields; mark holes.  
- Answer the four questions per cluster.  
- Dedupe URLs; update `repeat_count`.  
- Label `noise` / `watch` / `theme` / `killed`.  
- Force at least two kills by end of Day 3.  
- Promote at most three themes to `fire_test_next`.

Use AI if you want for drafts of the four questions. You still owe judgments. Automation does not speak in the pond as you.

**Output:** cleaned ledger · ≥2 kills · ≤3 fire-test candidates.

**Fail check:** 50 hopeful rows, zero kills → you are collecting, not deciding.

---

## Day 4 — Fire Test + JTBD

For each `fire_test_next` candidate (max three):

1. Fill `templates/fire-test.md` with risks first.  
2. Decision: PASS / FAIL / MORE SIGNAL.  
3. For every PASS, write `templates/jtbd-one-liner.md` and competing hires.  
4. Kill or demote anything that cannot hold a job sentence.

You should end Day 4 with **zero or one** PASS that has a job. Two PASSes means you are avoiding choice—pick a battlefield.

**Output:** fire pages · job one-liner for the survivor · forbidden_scope draft.

**Fail check:** PASS without risks or without “grab today.”

---

## Day 5 — Four Sources, One Card

Build one opportunity card (Chapter 6) for the survivor.

- Pain sources (≥2 independent).  
- Market behavior or explicit `none yet`.  
- Competitor scars in concrete words.  
- Tech window + open-source gap (no “not checked” if you want presale).  
- Risks ≥3.  
- Wedge one-liner rhyming with JTBD.  
- Status: `ready_for_presale` or back to kill/watch.

Run the thirty-minute chain pass if you are stalling. Then stop polishing. Cards are not novels.

**Output:** one card marked ready—or a kill with reason.

**Fail check:** ready_for_presale with open-source unchecked.

---

## Days 6–7 — Sell Air + Verdict

Execute Chapter 7 compressed:

**Day 6:** page + real price + payment path + delivery story (manual OK).  
**Day 7 morning/afternoon:** traffic from one/two ponds; collect questions; do not spam-ban yourself.  
**Day 7 evening — verdict meeting (30–45 min):**

Write:

```text
SPRINT VERDICT — date: ____
Theme:
Job one-liner:
What we offered (price + promise):
What moved (money / commits / clicks / silence):
Risks (≥3) now:
Decision: kill | revise & new sprint date | proceed to smallest ship
If proceed: broom scope (1 sentence) + forbidden_scope
If kill: public kill sentence
Chapter 8 failure modes we flirted with:
```

Celebrate kills. They are sprint outputs. Celebrate payments without deleting forbidden_scope.

**Output:** live test · dated verdict · next gate.

## What If the Sprint “Fails”?

It did not fail if it produced a clean kill or a sharp revise. It failed if it produced fog: no verdict, a secret half-build, a waitlist without a number, or a story that “the market is wrong” without artifacts.

Common end states:

- **Kill:** return to Day 1 with a different pond or accept a quiet month of not forcing a product.  
- **Revise:** change job, price, pond, or wedge; schedule the next sprint; do not keep the old page on life support without a date.  
- **Proceed:** enter Book 2 territory with a broom, not a museum.

---

## After the Gate — And Only After

Once money or strong commits say go, you still do not open the Inspiration Pool. You ship the smallest thing that makes the promise observable and collect payment without mythology. Conversion fine-tuning, ad scaling, SEO systems, GEO, and link trust belong later in the series. Using them as substitutes for Days 1–7 is how people buy traffic for a job that never stood.

Product taste still matters—after the gate. Before the gate, taste is often fear wearing aesthetics.

It merges the spirit of the handbook’s development and payment volumes—without promising that shipping small equals getting rich.

## Sprint Variants (When Life Is Not a Clean Calendar)

**Three-day compress:** Day1 pond+ledger sample → Day2 fire+JTBD+card → Day3 page+verdict. Only for experienced users of the method; still keep red lines.

**Async team sprint:** assign pond captain, fire captain, page captain; daily 15-minute sync; shared kill list. Avoid parallelizing into three different products.

**Service-first sprint:** if your air is a done-with-you service, Day6–7 may be outbound to ten named prospects with a one-page SOW. Same verdict logic; different surface.

Do not invent a variant whose real purpose is avoiding checkout. Variants are for constraints, not cowardice.

### Materials checklist (evening before Day 1)

- Blank ledger  
- Fire-test template  
- JTBD template  
- Opportunity card blank  
- A notes doc for public kills  
- A boring way to take payment  
- A timer  
- Chapter 8 red lines visible  

If the payment path is “we’ll figure it out on Day 6,” start figuring on Day 0. Mid-sprint checkout archaeology creates fake silence.

### Encouragement without prophecy

You might finish the week with a kill. That can be the best commercial decision available. You might finish with a payment. That is a beginning, not a destiny. Either way, you practiced judgment at the source of pain—the scarce skill Chapter 1 named. Keep the skill. Drop the theater.

*The Sea Playbook* continues when you are ready to ship small. Book 1’s work is complete when the Inspiration Wall no longer gets to pretend it is a gate.

## FAQ (Sprint)

**Q: Can I start midweek?**  
A: Yes. Rename days as steps. Keep the verdict date sacred.

**Q: What if my pond is tiny?**  
A: Tiny + specific beats huge + vague. If tiny and silent after an honest offer, that is still a verdict.

**Q: Can I hire help for Day 6 copy?**  
A: Yes, if they write from your fire page and job sentence—not from generic SaaS templates.

**Q: What if I get a yes from a big logo?**  
A: Great. Still write risks. Logo heat can be a single-source illusion with longer payment cycles. Use a paid pilot shape.

**Q: Do I need a company entity to sell air?**  
A: Follow your local rules. Method-wise, clarity and ability to deliver matter more than logo polish. Do not use entity delay as eternal digging.

## Worked Sprint Narrative (Composite Week)

**Monday:** You pick a design-ops adjacent forum and a plugin review surface. Ledger opens with fourteen raw links. Seeds get two AI-journal ideas so they stop polluting rows.  
**Tuesday:** Four-question pass clusters into three themes. Two meme piles become noise. One export theme repeats.  
**Wednesday:** Dedupe; kill a “pretty dashboard” theme; promote export + invoice-chase; kill invoice-chase after weak payment clues.  
**Thursday:** Fire PASS on export with three risks (support load, OSS libs exist, niche seasonality). JTBD written; competing hires include VA and screenshots.  
**Friday:** Card completed; OSS note says libs exist, shell does not; status ready_for_presale.  
**Saturday:** Page live at a flinch-worthy price; checkout tested; post with disclosure in one pond.  
**Sunday:** One stranger pays for setup; two ask for features in forbidden_scope; verdict = proceed with manual pipeline; forbidden_scope expanded; Book 2 entry criteria written.

Another week ends at Wednesday with two kills and no PASS. Also success. The narrative only matters as proof that days produce artifacts—not vibes.

### Energy management

Demand sprints are emotionally spiky. Schedule something non-product after the Day 7 verdict—walk, meal, unrelated hobby. Otherwise you will reopen the repo at midnight to soothe yourself. Soothing builds museums.

### Handoff note to future you

Write a 10-line handoff even if you are solo: pond, survivor job, price, what moved, risks, forbidden_scope, next book step. Future you will not remember the feelings. Future you will remember the file if it exists.



### Metrics for the sprint itself (process, not vanity)

Count only: sources logged, kills written, fire pages completed, cards completed, priced offers live, dated verdicts. Do not count words in the ledger as success. Do not count hours as virtue. A short week with a clean kill beats a long week with a beautiful fog deck.

If you run monthly sprints, keep a simple streak: consecutive weeks with a dated verdict. Broken streaks matter more than perfect frameworks. Restart without drama.

When Book 1’s draft sits beside Book 2’s empty folder, resist the urge to outline five products. One broom that survived air is enough work for a season.

Print the week table. Put it on the desk. When someone asks what you are working on this week, point at the day—not at a vision slide. Vision slides reopen the Inspiration Wall. Day numbers keep the broom in view.

## Day-5 Card Review Script (15 minutes with a partner or rubber duck)

Read the card aloud. Partner is allowed only these questions:

1. Which pain URLs can I open without your narration?  
2. Where is wallet weather—or the words `none yet`?  
3. What is the scar in concrete language?  
4. What did open source already do to your fixture?  
5. What are the three risks—said before the wedge?  
6. What is forbidden_scope, and where will buyers see the edge of the promise?

If any answer needs a story instead of a field, the card is not ready. Fix fields. Do not debate charisma.

## Pre-Sale Page Minimum (Day 6 without theater)

Your page only needs:

- Who is on fire (scene)  
- What you stop (one sentence)  
- What you will not do (forbidden_scope, short)  
- Price (real number)  
- How delivery works this month (manual OK)  
- How to pay  
- How to ask a question  
- A dated note that this is an early offer, not a forever roadmap poster  

Anything else is optional decoration. Decoration are how Day 6 becomes Day 20.

## Sprint Anti-Patterns (Quick List)

- Collecting 80 rows to avoid one kill  
- Designing logos on Day 1  
- Writing SEO calendars on Day 2  
- Building a monorepo on Day 4 “to demo the vision”  
- Waitlist without price on Day 6  
- Extending Day 7 into “another week of digging” without a revise date  
- Celebrating friend payments as stranger verdicts  
- Deleting forbidden_scope after first praise  

Pin this list next to Chapter 8’s red lines if your hands get itchy.

## B. Filled Fire Test (PASS-shaped composite)

```text
FIRE TEST — theme: Thursday markup export  date: 2026-11-04

1) Who is on fire (role + scene)?
   Design-ops / account lead packing client markups every Thursday afternoon before handoff.

2) What is the fire (one concrete sentence)?
   Export corrupts non-ASCII client names and burns 20–30 minutes of babysitting before the client call.

3) What do they grab today?
   Tool P or Q (partial), VA hours, or manual screenshot collage.

4) Cost of doing nothing for 7 more days?
   One more Thursday tax; client trust nick if a bad export ships; VA invoice continues.

5) Primary must-have shape: frequency

6) Would they accept an ugly broom that stops the fire? (yes/no + why)
   Yes — thread language prefers “just works on Thursday” over a redesign of the editor.

7) Cross-check: ≥2 independent sources? payment clue? open-source gap checked?
   Yes threads+reviews; paid substitutes+VA; libs exist, shell gap named.

8) Risks / holes (≥3 — FIRST):
   - Support load from encoding edge cases we underestimate
   - Technical buyers may stay on raw libs; our shell must earn convenience
   - Seasonality / client-mix changes could shrink Thursday intensity
   - Partial tools may ship a fix and collapse the gap

9) Decision: PASS (priced test next)

10) If PASS: smallest broom (one sentence)
    Hosted Thursday export with sane name encoding + checklist defaults.
    Forbidden scope: multiplayer editor, AI rewrite suite, “design OS,” mobile redraw.
```

**FAIL-shaped reminder:** if question 3 is “they grab nothing, they just wish,” or risks are blank, do not PASS. MORE SIGNAL with a timer beats a soft PASS.
