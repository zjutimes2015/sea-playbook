# The Shared Bridge

The kettle was already warm when June Zhou touched it.

Not the clean warmth of a fresh boil. The leftover kind — metal holding yesterday’s habit. The coworking kitchen smelled of wet grounds and the thin bite of vinegar she had poured the night before. Under the sink, the jug sat where she had left it, label facing out. She had turned it that way on purpose. People noticed labels more than messages.

The machine hissed when she opened the reservoir. A small, tired sound, like a bus door that no longer sealed. She filled it. Her thumb found the scratch on the kettle’s side — a dull silver river that caught the under-cabinet light. Warm. Almost comforting, if you didn’t know how many mornings had put that heat there.

Someone had left a mug in the sink again. Brown ring. Lipstick on the rim, the color of dried chili oil. June rinsed it. The water ran loud in the steel basin. She set the mug on the rack and did not look for a name on the bottom.

Her phone buzzed on the counter.

**Bay Kitchen**

Eric Tan: *Who fixed the coffee machine again? Legend!!*  
Three clap emojis. A coffee cup. A bowing sticker that had been overused since Spring Festival.

June wiped her hands on a towel that smelled faintly of lemon soap and old steam. She typed *np* and put the phone face down. The kettle clicked toward a boil. She watched the scratch instead of the chat.

Outside the kitchen’s half-open door, the coworking floor woke in pieces: chair wheels, a laugh cut short, the soft thud of a package on the reception desk. A delivery driver said a name wrong and waited. June poured the first water through without coffee, a rinse she had taught no one. Clear heat into the drip tray. She dumped it. Only then did she add grounds from the shared bag — half full, bought with her card last month, marked *community* in permanent ink that had smeared.

---

By ten, the kitchen had traffic.

A designer with noise-canceling headphones poured without looking. A guy in a Patagonia vest filmed his latte art for a story and did not wipe the drip tray. Sophie Wen came in late, backpack strap twisted, and said, “Oh — it’s working,” as if the machine were weather.

“Vinegar last night,” June said.

Sophie nodded too fast. “I was going to. Client call ran.” She poured, blew on the cup, burned her tongue anyway. “Thursday I’ll take a turn?”

“Sure,” June said.

Sophie smiled like a calendar invite that hadn’t been sent. Then her phone lit up and she was gone, cup trailing a thin ribbon of heat.

June scrubbed the drip tray. The burnt smell came up sharp when she tipped the grounds into the bin. Her knuckle brushed the kettle lip. Warm still. She filed the feeling somewhere private, next to the mug with the chili-oil stain and Eric’s clap emojis.

On the shared Notion board — *Community Ops* — the coffee rotation page had one name in the Assignee column.

Hers.

Kevin, who administered the WeChat group and loved a poll the way some people loved a pitch deck, had posted at midnight:

*Coffee machine care — who can own a day?*  
Options: Mon / Tue / Wed / Thu / Fri  
Three thumbs-up. No names under the days.

June had not voted. Voting felt like asking.

She opened the page history. Sophie’s cursor had hovered once last week and left. Eric had never opened it. The permissions list still included two people who had moved back to Shanghai in March. June removed the dead accounts. Cleanup counted as care. She did not add a second living name.

---

She shipped a pricing fix before lunch. The kind of work that left a clean diff and a quiet Slack. Useful. Contained. No one needed to clap. Her product did not leave lipstick on mugs. It did not need vinegar. She liked that about code — when it broke, the log said so without pretending to be grateful.

At 12:40 she went back for water. The kettle had cooled to the temperature of a laptop left open. She touched it without thinking — check, the way you check a lock. Scratch under the pad of her thumb. She refilled the reservoir for whoever came next. Habit wore a groove deeper than intention.

Eric passed through with a protein bar. “You’re a saint,” he said, not stopping. “One day I’ll learn the vinegar thing.”

“It’s under the sink,” June said. “Label out.”

“Legend,” he said again, already half in the hallway.

She almost said *Don’t call me that.* The words sat behind her teeth like a stone. She swallowed water instead. It tasted of the building’s pipes and the plastic filter she had ordered two weeks ago with her own card because the shared expenses spreadsheet still said *TBD*.

A woman from a climate startup asked if the oat milk was community. June pointed at the shelf. The oat milk was hers, unlabeled, because labels on milk started arguments. The woman poured freely. June watched the carton lighten and said nothing. Saying something would have been a kind of ask — or a kind of bill. She could not tell which she feared more.

---

Wednesday night the group chat bloomed without her.

Sophie: *I can take Thursday?* then, nineteen minutes later, *Sorry — client moved again. Next week!*  
Kevin: *no worries we can repoll*  
Eric: *June usually got us anyway haha*

June watched the thread from her studio apartment while a rice cooker clicked off. The apartment smelled of scallion and the dry heat of the heater kicking on. Rain tapped the window in an uneven meter. She did not reply. Replying would have been asking in a costume.

She opened Notion. Sophie’s name had appeared under Thursday for eleven minutes according to version history. Then blank again. June left the blank. She added a line to the page in small gray text: *Filter: Melitta 4. Vinegar: under sink.* Then she deleted the gray text. Documentation without an audience felt like talking into a vent.

She boiled water in her own small kettle at home. Different scratch — a factory seam, not a wound. Different heat. She drank standing up, phone dark on the counter, screen waking now and then with light that made the scallion smell seem briefly like an office.

Her mother called on video, evening in another timezone. "Eaten yet?"  
"Yes."  
"How's the company?"  
"Fine."  
Her mother could hear the short answers and did not push. On the wall behind June, the calendar was empty except for a dentist square. The WeChat group badge glowed red at the edge of the laptop. June did not tilt the camera toward it. Some bridges you did not show parents. They would ask why you were the concrete.

---

Thursday the coworking kettle was too hot.

Someone had run it dry and refilled late. The metal held a sharp, unfriendly heat. When June lifted it to rinse Eric’s abandoned AeroPress — he had left the rubber seal stuck to the counter like a black comma — her knuckle kissed the lip.

The burn was small. A white bead. Then pink.

She ran cold water. The faucet knocked once in the wall, a hollow pipe sound. The kitchen smelled of scorched grounds and the sweet chemical of dish soap. Her skin pulsed under the stream. She dried her hand and posted in the group:

*Tip: don’t run the kettle empty. Reservoir mark is on the left. Vinegar under sink if it tastes bitter.*

No speech. No ledger made public.

Eric replied in forty seconds: *Legend!!*  
Kevin hearted the tip.  
Sophie sent a sticker of a hamster bowing.

June looked at the burn. She did not photograph it. Photographing would have been a kind of ask.

She stayed an extra twenty minutes and wiped the cabinet faces. Sticky film from someone else’s protein spill. The cloth dragged with a soft resistance. Under the sink she squared the vinegar jug again, label out, and noticed the Melitta box had been opened from the wrong end. She turned it. Small corrections. The kitchen accepted them without thanks, which was honest.

At 4:10 a Slack DM arrived from Alex, the space manager, who wrote in the tone of someone forwarding a package:

*Hey June — machine overflowed a bit Tuesday night after you left early for that call. Mopped what we could. Assuming you’ll do the usual wipe / descale when you’re in? You’re the best with that thing :)*

Tuesday she had left for an investor call. She had told no one in Bay Kitchen. The flood was a sentence she was expected to finish.

She typed: *Can we put kitchen ops on the community rota with a name besides mine?*  
Deleted it.  
Typed: *Sure.*  
Sent.

The kettle, when she checked it before leaving, still radiated. Heat with nowhere to go. She did not touch the scratch. In the glass of the microwave door she saw her face for a second — calm, useful, a little tired around the mouth — and looked away before the reflection could become a conversation.

---

Friday there was a founder mixer in the glass room by the printers. Someone had written on the whiteboard: *Shared stacks — what actually works?*

June stood near the back with a paper cup from the cafe downstairs. She had bought it on purpose. The cafe coffee tasted flatter, honest in its mediocrity. No vinegar ghost. No scratch. The lid popped softly when she opened it. Steam smelled of roasted beans and the cardboard sleeve.

A man with a seed round in his voice pointed at her. “June runs the Notion for the Chinese founders group, right? And the machine? That’s what I mean by infrastructure.”

People laughed politely. Infrastructure. As if she were a pipe.

Kevin waved from a chair. “She’s our bridge!”

June smiled the smile she used on product demos when a feature was half ready. She did not say that the Notion password had never been rotated. She did not say that “bridge” meant she stood in water so other people could stay dry. She answered two questions about filter brands and one about why polls failed. Her knuckle still shone pink under the fluorescent light.

Someone asked how to get people to share maintenance. June said, “Put a name on a day. Not a vibe.” A few people nodded as if she had quoted a book. She had not. She had quoted the empty Assignee cell in her head.

Afterward Sophie caught her by the snack shelf. The shelf smelled of opened granola and the dust that settled on everything sweet. “I meant Thursday. I really did.”

“I know,” June said. And she did. Meaning was cheap in this building. Calendar blocks were not.

Eric appeared with two cans of sparkling water and offered her one. Cold. Condensation on her unburned fingers. “Mixer coffee is trash,” he said. “Yours is better.”

“It isn’t mine,” June said.

He laughed, missing the seam in the sentence. “You know what I mean.”

She did. That was the problem.

---

That night she washed her mug at home with more care than it needed. The sponge scratched softly. Steam rose from the tap into her face, clean, without vinegar. She thought of the coworking scratch catching light and felt a pull she refused to name as anything but unfinished work. Unfinished work she understood. She could schedule it. She could close it. The pull was not a ticket.

She opened the shared expenses sheet. *Coffee supplies — TBD.* She typed her own name in the Paid By column for the Melitta order, then the vinegar, then the oat milk she had never labeled. The total was small. The smallness annoyed her more than a large number would have. Large numbers justified themselves. Small ones looked like hobbies.

---

She stopped on Saturday without a thread, without a poll, without a speech.

On the walk to the library a man held a door for her without looking up from his phone. She said thanks; he did not hear. The city was full of small unpaid assists. She had made a career of noticing them and a habit of collecting them.

Saturday she worked from a library branch that smelled of paper and wet umbrellas. Fluorescent lights hummed a steady note above the fiction stacks. She sat near a window that looked onto a parking lot. When she needed caffeine she walked to a cart on the corner. The cart’s espresso machine screamed in short bursts. The cup warmed her hands through the sleeve. No scratch. No ledger.

Sunday she stayed in and refactored a billing webhook until the tests went green. Rain again, softer. The rice cooker stayed cold; she ate noodles from a bag. She bought cafe coffee both days. Paper sleeves. The burn on her knuckle dulled to a tight itch she pressed absently while reading logs. She noticed she was keeping a different ledger now: hours since she had touched the coworking kettle. The count rose like a progress bar she did not want to complete.


Her phone filled anyway.

Kevin: *machine tasting weird anyone free?*  
Eric: *I poured some vinegar?? did I do too much lol*  
Sophie: *I can try Monday morning before standup*  
Unknown number in the group (someone’s cousin visiting): *is there coffee?*  
Alex (forwarded into the chat by Kevin): *Please don’t leave sticky notes on the machine — use the board :)*

June read. She made toast. The toaster sprang with a sound like a cheap lock. Butter melted into the holes. She stood at the counter and did not open Notion. The heater clicked on; warm air pushed the smell of toast toward the hallway.

On Sunday night she almost went in. Keys in hand. Jacket half on. The thought of the reservoir sitting empty pulled at her the way a failing job pulls at a pager. She pictured the scratch in the dark kitchen, cold, waiting for a thumb that knew it. She set the keys down. Asking for help and refusing to give it were not opposites. They were the same refusal wearing different coats.

Between midnight and the draft she replayed Alex’s Slack in the dark: *You’re the best with that thing.* Best was a compliment that functioned like an assignment. She turned onto her side. The sheet felt cool where her bare shoulder left it. Outside, a scooter whined up the hill and faded.

She slept badly. The heater clicked. Somewhere a neighbor’s door chain rattled. At 3 a.m. she drafted a message:

*I need someone else to own the machine for two weeks.*

She stared at the word *need*. It looked foreign in her own typing. She deleted the draft. Deleting felt like competence. That should have warned her.

---

Monday the kitchen looked like a meeting that had ended without minutes.

Sticky notes on the machine in three handwritings:

*We’ll rotate!!*  
*Vinegar? ?*  
*Do not touch — waiting for June*

The scratched kettle sat cold on a stack of unpaid print invoices someone had moved from the admin desk. Paperweight. The metal held no heat at all. When June lifted it, the scratch was just a scratch — no river of light, no leftover habit. Cold enough to feel like a sink’s underside. The smell was stale grounds and the glue of sticky notes and a sour edge where vinegar had been poured straight into the grounds basket by a hopeful hand.

Eric’s AeroPress seal was back on the counter, black comma.

Someone had printed Kevin’s poll and taped it to the fridge. The paper curled at one corner. Two handwritten initials in the margin, neither hers. Progress, if you squinted. The fridge hummed, steady, indifferent. When she opened it for no reason, cold air met the sour vinegar smell and made a third smell that belonged to kitchens that had been argued over.


Sophie stood at the doorway with her backpack. “I came. I didn’t know the order. The steps.”

“Under the sink,” June said. Then she saw the jug on the counter, half empty, cap off. “Usually.”

Sophie flushed. “Eric moved it.”

Of course he had. People moved what they didn’t plan to put back.

Kevin walked in reading his phone. “There she is. We held a poll—”  
“I saw,” June said.

He glanced at the sticky notes, then at her knuckle, then away. “We can make a rota that sticks this time.”

“Put names,” June said. “Not thumbs.”

He nodded, already composing a message. Nodding was his second language.

June set the kettle in the sink. Ran water. The cold climbed her wrists. She descaled the way she always had — measured, quiet, the motions of a person repairing a tool she no longer wanted to own. The machine hissed again, that bus-door sound. Steam lifted. For a minute the kitchen smelled almost right: wet coffee, clean acid, hot metal returning.

She scrubbed the lip. Dried the scratch with the lemon towel. Left the Melitta box on the counter where a face would see it. Wrote on a single sticky note, short enough for a phone screen:

*Steps on Notion. Vinegar dose: 1:1 with water. Don’t pour on grounds. Filter brand on the box.*

She did not write her name.

While the clean water ran through, she listened: hiss, then the softer tick of cooling metal. Sophie washed two mugs without being asked. The clink of ceramic on the rack was careful, almost shy. Eric did not appear. His absence had a shape June recognized — light, friendly, elsewhere.

On her laptop at the high table facing the window, she opened Community Ops. Assignee: June Zhou. She cleared the field. The cell went empty — a small white absence. She added Kevin and Sophie as can-edit. She reset the password to a phrase she put in the page header, plain text, almost rude in its clarity. Then she logged out of the owner session.

No announcement in WeChat. The empty cell would have to speak, or not.

She poured the first working cup into a paper sleeve from downstairs, not a house mug. The coffee tasted of vinegar at the edges. She drank it anyway. Heat through paper was different from heat through scratched steel. Less intimate. She could put it down.

Alex messaged: *You’re in! Machine alive again? You’re a lifesaver.*

June looked at the pink ghost on her knuckle. Typed: *Password and steps are on the Notion page. I’m off the assignee list.*  
Sent before she could soften it.

Alex: *Got it — I’ll ask the group to cover*  
Which meant he would ask her again next month with different emoji.

She rinsed the kettle once more and set it back on its base, empty of invoices, empty of other people’s paper. Warmth returning slowly. Not hers to keep.

---

On the sidewalk the wind brought frying oil from the dumpling place two doors down and the wet dust of a street being swept. Her paper cup cooled fast. Traffic noise came in layers: a truck reverse-beep, a bicycle bell, someone’s headphone leak of a podcast laughing. A leaf stuck to her shoe; she scraped it off on the curb. The scrape was louder than she expected.

The group chat unlocked itself in her pocket.

Sophie: *I took Monday. Notes printed.*  
Eric: *I can do filters I think*  
Kevin: *poll rebuilt please vote for real this time*  
Someone June barely knew: *added a checklist*

June stopped under the awning. She opened the checklist. Wrong vinegar ratio. Filter size off by one. A cheerful checkbox labeled *ask June if unsure*.

Her thumb hovered.

She typed a correction — precise, kind, the old bridge rebuilding itself out of habit. The sentence looked like her. Useful. Contained. Necessary.

She held the delete key until the bubble emptied.

The cost arrived without language: a lightening that was also a drop, as if she had set down a kettle she had forgotten she was carrying. Across the street the coworking windows threw back a flat morning sky. Somewhere inside, metal would cool again. Someone would touch the scratch and feel only steel.

A notification badge returned. She did not open it. The effort of not opening it was physical — jaw, thumb, the small muscle near her eye.

June walked toward the cafe that sold mediocre coffee in honest paper. She checked once, a block later, against her own promise, and saw two votes on Kevin’s poll. Better than zero. Not enough for a bridge.

She let the phone sleep. The cup warmed her palm a little, then less. She drank. The taste was ordinary.

At the corner she almost turned back — one more wipe, one more label facing out, one more gray note no one would read. Her feet did not take the turn. A bus sighed to a stop beside her; doors opened with a seal-sound that was not the machine and still reminded her of it. She did not board. She kept walking, past the dumpling oil and the swept dust, toward a morning that did not need her to boil first.
