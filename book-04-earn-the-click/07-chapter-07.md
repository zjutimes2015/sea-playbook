# Chapter 7 — Being Linked Is Not Being Recommended

The Organic board acquired a new column: **Backlinks**.

A vendor deck sat next to it. The deck promised a domain rating in a round number, a package of “niche editorials,” and a timeline that looked like a product launch. A teammate had already exported a competitor’s referring-domain list and highlighted everything that looked cheap to copy. The builder, still lonely, wrote a note: *If we get linked, we get ranked. If we get ranked, the street notices.*

A page on a forgotten directory already pointed at Thursday Packet. The anchor text was the homepage title. The surrounding sentence was leftover Latin. The directory was stored in a search index and otherwise unread. The builder had counted it as one. The vendor would have called it a start.

It was a mention the way a photocopier is an audience.

**A link is a path on the web.** Sometimes it is also a recommendation: a person, in a real sentence, sending a reader to a document that helps. Search systems and answer systems have spent years trying to tell those two apart, and they are not finished, and they never will be finished in a way that makes a package of editorials safe. Your job is simpler than their job. Your job is to stop treating a count as trust.

---

## Why Off-Site Mentions Still Matter (And When They Do Not)

I will not tell you that links are dead. I will not tell you they are a slot machine.

The handbook’s reasons for needing *some* off-site recognition are ordinary:

1. **Trust.** A document nobody ever points to is harder for a system to trust, especially when it is new.  
2. **Discovery.** A new URL can be found because a living page mentioned it. There are teaching stories of a thoughtful public submission being followed by a faster crawl. Stories are not a timetable.  
3. **Entity.** Journalists, partners, product directories, and documentation sets help a system decide that Thursday Packet is a thing in the world, not a string.  
4. **A side door into answer systems.** Many models meet you first on a third-party page. That is Chapter 6’s corroboration point, not a reason to buy a profile and call it GEO.

You do *not* need “links at all costs” when the owner URL is still blocked, still thin, still isolated inside your own site, or still a suitcase of jobs. In those weeks, off-site work is perfume on a document the librarian has not filed.

Need is not superstition. Superstition is what happens when the board only has a count.

## Five Steps Before You Ask for a Path

This brake is the same in the SEO handbook and the links handbook, and I will not let a vendor skip it.

| Step | Question | A pass looks like | If you fail |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Basics | Is the URL focused, canonical, crawlable, and free of soft 404s and leftover noindex? | Chapter 4’s gate is boring | Go back to the technical card |
| 2. Content | Does the document have an increment the current results lack? | Chapter 5’s sentence exists and the last-click holds | Deepen or merge; do not buy a mention |
| 3. SERP gap | Are you losing because the page is weaker, or because the occupants also have a history of being believed? | You can say which | Content gap: write. Trust gap: then, and only then, plan mentions |
| 4. Internal paths | Do the homepage, a hub, and related documents send people and crawlers here with honest anchors? | The URL is not an orphan | Fix your own votes first |
| 5. Off-site plan | What kind of mention, at what pace, with what observation window? | A quality bar and a review date | If you cannot write the window, you are shopping |

A useful companion, already named in the handbook, is the **middle-door page**: a resource that is naturally citable — a guide, a small tool, a dated test — receives the mention, and then points, honestly, at the page that can take a payment. This relieves the false demand that every link land on a pricing URL. It is not a cloak for a scheme. The resource has to be a resource.

Another companion is a **nameable concept** you actually use, which other people can mention without feeling like they swallowed an ad. That is a content strategy. It is not a cluster of hidden pages.

After a mention appears, wait long enough to see whether it is stored, whether it refers a person, and whether a query moved. A rising third-party “rating” is not the same as the owner query moving. If you only watch the rating, you will buy more of whatever moved the rating.

## Quality Is Context, Not a Score

A link is worth talking about when three things are true enough:

1. **The source can be believed** in its neighborhood. A sentence in a respected technical article is not the same object as a footer swap.  
2. **The source is about a nearby job.** A typography journal pointing at name repair is a different object from a casino blog pointing at the same URL. In an answer-engine world, “who you stand next to” gets louder, not quieter.  
3. **The mention lives in a sentence that does a job.** In-body, specific, surround by meaning. Sidebars, footers, and bulk directories trend weaker as recommendations, even when they remain paths.

Everything else is commentary: follow or nofollow or sponsored (paid arrangements should not costume themselves as affection), whether the page stays live and stored, whether the anchor language is varied and non-robotic, how many distinct sites talk about you rather than how many times one site repeats you.

If a vendor will not discuss those three, they are selling a count.

## Paths You Can Defend, and Paths You Should Side-Eye

I will not pretend there is one funnel.

**Digital PR and genuine media.** Slow, often deserved only when you have a finding, a data set, or a story a reporter already wants. Platforms that connect experts to journalists exist and change names. Buying a fake “placement” and calling it PR is a later scandal with a receipt.

**Partners.** Case studies, integrations, and co-written notes with people you actually work with. Real relationships first.

**Citable assets.** A working tool, a dated report, a careful guide. You build the asset, then you tell the truth about it in rooms that already care. This is the path that fits Thursday Packet best: the preview is the asset.

**Lists and co-citations.** Being named next to tools in the same job, on a page a person would read. Begging to be on every “top 50” list is a job I do not wish for you. One accurate list is worth a stack of junk.

**Classic tactics, used as tactics:** guest writing when you have a real article for a real audience; resource-page requests when your page is actually a resource; repairing a broken link when you have the better living document. Each of these dies when it becomes a mass mail.

**HARO-class and journalist queries,** when you can answer as a person who did the work, not as a brand account with a link in the first sentence.

**Platforms and profiles.** Product Hunt, G2-like directories, GitHub, a well-filled company profile, a community wiki. The job is not “drop the URL.” The job is to make it possible for a system and a person to see who you are, what you replace, and what you refuse to do. Empty profiles are wallpaper.

What I want you to refuse by default: buying bulk editorials, swapping links as a program, automating submissions to thousands of sites, inventing authors, or dressing a paid mention as a love letter. Search policies move, but the spirit does not: **deceptive off-site promotion is a future cleanup bill.** Marketplace research — looking at what exists — is not the same as shopping a ranking. If you ever pay for a mention, expect a sponsored or nofollow treatment, and do not forecast a position.

## Automation, If You Must, Knows Its Place

A bot can check whether a URL is still live, whether a directory form is a duplicate, or whether a competitor acquired a new mention. A person decides the quality bar, the pitch, and the no-list. A bot that signs up, pays, or invents a biography has already crossed the line this book will not cross.

If automation and quality seem to fight, quality wins, and the sprint gets slower. That is an adult outcome.

## Thirty Days, as a Skeleton, Not a Warranty

If you want a month-shaped plan after the five steps pass, the handbook’s skeleton is sane:

- Week 1: diagnosis, internal paths, a small pack of checkable facts you can hand to a partner or a writer.  
- Week 2: complete the profiles that are real, and evaluate one or two high-quality placements you would still respect if they said no.  
- Week 3: a living library of competitor mentions (the ones that repeat are more interesting than the one-off oddities), and a limited, human submission list. Daily caps exist so you cannot trance.  
- Week 4: one citable asset, or one deep community write-up, plus a review of what was stored and what referred a person.

The month’s motto is the one I want on the vendor deck: **do not lower the quality bar, do not rush the rhythm, and do not bet the month on a ranking promise.**

Forbidden sentences, which I want you to treat as red lines:

- “Submit to these directories and you will be on page one.”  
- “When the rating hits X, traffic is guaranteed.”  
- “Purchased guest posts are risk-free, white-hat, and sufficient.”  
- “An automated thousand-site day will do for you what it did in a screenshot.”

## Where Link Advice Falsifies Itself

**A famous brand can live for a long time on direct type-in and branded search**, with fewer public paths than a new tool. Do not copy their indifference on day one, and do not copy their mentions by scraping.

**A genuinely viral moment can look like “mentions we did not buy.”** It is usually a citable asset plus a room that already existed (Book 3). You cannot schedule it. You can be fit for it.

**Some nofollow or sponsored mentions still send people**, and people can pay you. Count the referral and the journey. Do not pretend the attribute is a moral failure.

**You can over-earn mentions for a page that cannot finish the job.** Then Chapter 5 was the real failure, and Chapter 8 will say so again.

---

## Takeaway

**Rule:** A path on the web is not automatically a recommendation. Diagnose basics, increment, SERP gap, and internal votes before you ask for a mention.

**This week:** Run the five steps on the owner URL. If you fail steps 1–4, close the vendor tab. If you pass, write one defensible request or one partner note — not a list of a hundred.
