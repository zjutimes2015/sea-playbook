# Chapter 6 — Being Seen Is Not Being Chosen

A Thursday Packet builder, late in the week, typed a question into a public chatbot.

“How do I keep client names readable when typography breaks in an export?”

The answer was fluent. It mentioned Unicode, a generic converter, and a well-known design tool. It did not mention Thursday Packet. The builder tried again, with the product name in the question. This time the model described a “document export helper” in a sentence that could have been anyone, then pointed at a directory page that had scraped the homepage title two months earlier. A teammate, trying to be kind, said: “We’re being seen. That’s the beginning of GEO.”

It was not a beginning. It was a rumor of a beginning.

**Generative engine optimization**, or whatever name the year is using, is not a reskin of “get the tenth blue link.” It is a second axis: when an answer is assembled, are you an eligible source, and are you the source that gets chosen? Eligibility is not a trophy. Choice is not a purchase. The click, if it happens, still has to finish the Thursday.

I will use GEO as a shorthand because the handbook does. I will not pretend the acronym is stable, or that a green score in a third-party checker means an answer engine loves you.

---

## Two Axes, Not a Replacement Religion

The useful contrast is a table you can steal, then distrust wherever a row becomes a slogan.

| Dimension | Classical search | Answer engines (GEO as a job) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What you are fighting for | A position in a list | A mention, a citation, or a place in a synthesized answer |
| What the person does | Search, click, sort | Ask, read an answer, sometimes click, often ask a follow-up |
| What your page is for | A ranking document | A lift-able, checkable fragment, plus a reason to open the full page |
| Language | Stems, tails, intent pages | Full questions, entities, and the subtopics a follow-up will need |
| Off-site emphasis | Links and retrieved endorsements | Mentions at trusted nodes, third-party corroboration |
| Early metrics | Impressions, clicks, stays | Citation share, branded search, referrals that actually arrive — and manual probes |
| Technical emphasis | Speed, mobile, indexation | Reachability for the relevant bots, clear structure, a machine-readable map of the site |
| Unit of competition | A handful of visible links | A pool of candidate sources, often steeper at the head |
| How money still happens | A visit, then a path | Attention and trust first; the product still has to close later |

The handbook’s settled stance, and mine, is not “GEO replaces SEO.” It is: **use classical search to stay in the retrieval pool; use citability work to improve the conditions under which an answer system might pick you; promise neither.**

There is a second, quieter stance in the source archive that I also want on the table: for some products, this is less a traffic strategy than a *recognition* strategy. Referral share from answer engines may stay small for a long time. The people who do arrive may arrive with a sharper intent. That is a shape worth watching. It is not a reason to abandon the typography room, the owned hook, or the page that finishes the job.

## Three Changes in How People Ask

If you only write fragment titles — “export typography,” “PDF names” — you are still writing for a world of ten blue links and a punchy result.

People now type, and talk, in **scene questions**: “I have never shipped a client export. The names look wrong. Where do I start?” An answer system likes complete questions because that is what it is asked.

The second change is **presentation**. The screen may give an answer with sources, or an answer with no useful source, before it gives you a list. Being cited is not automatically a loss of the visit. It can also be how a later branded search begins. Being omitted, when you actually had the increment, is the part that should hurt.

The third change is **follow-up**. A person who asked about broken names will ask about ligatures, about a script you do not support, about whether the preview is safe to use on a confidential file. A site that only owns one shallow definition will fall out of the conversation. A site that owns a tight cluster of true, related documents has a chance to be useful twice.

The content pressure splits in a way that should sound like Chapter 5. Standard answers — “what is a PDF?” — are easy for a model to give without you. **Experience, cases, and checkable numbers are harder to invent**, and they are the reason a person still opens a full page. If your GEO plan is to publish more dictionary entries, you are volunteering to be unnecessary.

## Seen Versus Chosen

A lot of tools now sell a score. They will tell you that you lack an FAQ, that your “GEO score” is 89 or 95, that a competitor is “more visible.” I have watched teams chase the missing FAQ module and ship six questions the page had already answered, in worse prose, because a checker wanted a green box.

The source archive is not of one mind, and I will not pretend it is. Many notes argue for FAQ schema and a question-shaped module. A more careful note says that a low-quality FAQ can make the document noisier and that **only a small share of retrieved pages become citations**. The deciding pieces, in that telling, include semantic fit, a clear URL, structure a machine can parse, and — often underestimated — **third-party mentions that are stronger than self-description**.

The gate I will keep is not “did the checker go green.” It is: **can a system correctly understand you, and is there a fragment worth lifting?** If you cannot point at the sentences, the score is theater.

## Citability Density, in Ordinary Language

SEO, at its best, asks whether a human would stay and use the page. GEO, at its best, asks whether a machine, after a fast pass, can **take a complete sentence with a clear boundary** and use it in an answer without having to invent your limit.

**Citability density** is a teaching name for the share of sentences that could be lifted that way. Two pages on the same site, written with the same care for “readability,” can split: one is a warm essay whose conclusions live in the middle of paragraphs; the other leaves a compact, standalone sentence every few sections. The second is easier to use. That does not make it better for a human by default. It makes it easier to cite *if the sentence is true*.

If your score is high and your citations are zero, do not add more words. Change the sentences.

### Four sentence types that tend to lift

The handbook’s working list, from practice notes rather than from a law of nature:

1. **Definitions.** “X is…” when X is actually yours to define, or when you are defining a boundary, not reciting an encyclopedia.  
2. **Measured statements.** A number, a before and after, and a source or a method when you have one. A handbook aside says that “data plus a source” has looked like a strong return in some tests. Treat that as a method hint, not as your forecast.  
3. **Steps.** First, second, third — when the job is how-to, and the steps work.  
4. **Contrast that is already a judgment.** “This is faster to run and still needs a human check,” or “this repairs ligatures and still fails on the following scripts.” A framework a person could reuse.

The injuries are predictable. The only new sentence is too deep. There is no boundary, so a model will invent one. There is no question-shaped entry for a real question a user asked. Trust is thin, and a more checkable competitor is sitting in the candidate pool.

A practical habit: **every few sections, leave one compressed sentence that can stand alone.** Then ask, unkindly: which sentences could be lifted today? If you cannot answer, the page is not ready for this chapter’s job.

## Answer Pages: Conclusion, Reason, Detail

Classical essays like to warm up. Answer systems, and hurried people, do not.

Write the lift-able conclusion near the top — in the first few sentences, short enough to peel off. Then the reason. Then the detail, the test, the limitation. Then a brief close that does not undo the conclusion. Titles can be complete questions when that is how people ask: “How do I keep client names readable in a typography export in 2026?” is more usable than “Export excellence.”

FAQs, when you use them, should be **real questions in a person’s mouth**, five to eight if you truly have them, each with a first sentence that is already an answer, short enough to remain a fragment, and numbered when you have a number. Fifty to two hundred words is a teaching range, not a prison. Rebuild your best existing pages first — the useful fifth, not the rotting attic.

Self-check: if you cannot point at the lift-able sentences, you are not done. If you added a FAQ to please a checker and the answers repeat the page without increment, delete it.

## Robots, llms.txt, and Schema — With Their Boundaries

Crawler policy is a choice with costs, not a moral costume.

Open your `robots.txt` and see whether you accidentally blocked **search and user-triggered answer crawlers**. Names move. People now talk about Googlebot, Bingbot, GPTBot, PerplexityBot, ClaudeBot, OAI-SearchBot, and others. Recheck the official lists. The handbook records two practices: open the door widely, which is simpler; or **allow retrieval-and-answer crawlers and be selective about training crawlers**. The second practice comes with a point I will underline: blocking a training crawler is not the same as disappearing from AI search; blocking a search or answer crawler can be a vanishing act.

For product sites in this series, my bias, when policy and privacy allow, is to **protect the answer path first** and to write down the training decision so it is not remade in a panic every quarter. Also keep ordinary indexation healthy. Some answer paths still rely on an ordinary web index. If you hide from Bing and hope for a chatbot, you may have misunderstood the plumbing. Recheck; plumbing changes.

**llms.txt** is a proposed, plain-text map of a site for language models, associated with Jeremy Howard and the fast.ai direction of work: a Markdown file at the root that tells a model which documents matter. It is early. Adoption is uneven. A file full of “click to learn more,” or a file that hides the important URLs under a vague optional list, is a performance. Do not expect a file to force a citation. Do expect it to stop wasting a model’s first look, if anything looks at all.

**Schema** is a more mature cousin: a machine-readable description of the page you already showed a human. Article, FAQPage, HowTo, BreadcrumbList, Organization — used when they are true. Test with the official rich-result tools. Fake FAQ schema on a page without those questions is a lie, and it is the kind of lie that gets cleaned up in public.

### Entities, and the humility of sameAs

A model, and a search system, often need to know that Thursday Packet is an organization, not just another string in a blog. That is the entity job. An Organization (or Person) description, consistent names, and `sameAs` links to profiles you actually control — a GitHub, a documentation domain, a company page that is real — help a system join the dots.

Third-party mentions still beat a lonely official paragraph. A directory with a stale one-line scrape is a weak sameAs. A partner write-up that describes the job accurately is a stronger one. You cannot fake the second with twelve self-published bios.

## A Weekly Probe, and What Not to Count as a Sale

Once a week, for ten or fifteen minutes, ask the systems your buyers actually use a small set of questions you do not change every time: the scene question, a follow-up, and your brand name. Record whether you are omitted, mentioned without a link, cited, or described wrongly. Wrong is useful. It is a documentation bug.

Layer the metrics:

- **Technical:** were the relevant crawlers allowed? Did the sitemap and llms.txt still tell the truth?  
- **Document:** did the owner URL keep its lift-able sentences?  
- **Recognition:** did a probe mention you? Did a third party?  
- **Business:** did anyone arrive and finish a preview or a payment?

A mention in a probe is not a sale. A branded search after a mention is interesting. A referral that bounces is Chapter 5 again. Keep the layers labeled or you will decorate a rumor.

The handbook mentions product-shaped tools that try to automate probes, schema, or “GEO scores,” including work in the same family as the operator’s own experiments. Use them, if you use them, as benches. Do not let a vendor dashboard become a personality. Open-source helpers can draft and audit. They cannot own the increment.

**Scene question we want to be eligible for:** “How do I keep client names readable when typography breaks in a Thursday export?”

**Lift-able conclusion (first three sentences):** Client names break in exports when ligatures and unusual letterforms are flattened. **Other lift-able sentences:** one measured before/after on character error rates for a dated sample of twelve files (sample size stated); a four-step check a person can run without the product; a contrast sentence about what a generic converter will not see.

## Where GEO Advice Falsifies Itself

**If nobody asks the question in answer engines, you are optimizing a stage that is empty.** Keep the classical page honest. Do not build a citation theater.

**Some answers will never cite a small product** because official documentation or a standard already closes the job. That is a 0-score situation in a new costume. Accept it.

**A mention can be defamatory or simply wrong.** Correct it where you can — on your page, in your docs, with a civil note to a webmaster if a directory scraped garbage. Do not celebrate a wrong mention as “visibility.”

**A media site may want citation share more than a product journey.** Say so. This book’s named reader is still trying to finish a Thursday.

**Standards for llms.txt and for bot names will move.** If a later official convention replaces what I described, follow the official page, not this paragraph.

---

## Takeaway

**Rule:** Being retrieved into a candidate pool is not being chosen in an answer, and being chosen is not a finished job.

**This week:** Rewrite the first three sentences of the owner URL so they can be lifted without inventing your boundary. Run one unchanged probe. Record omission, mention, citation, or error — not a vibe.
