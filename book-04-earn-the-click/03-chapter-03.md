# Chapter 3 — Words You Can Defend

The keyword tool had already picked a favorite for Thursday Packet: a fat phrase with a volume that looked like a city.

The builder did not love the phrase. It did not sound like the typography-and-exports thread. It sounded like every other product that turns a file into another file. But the number was large, the difficulty score was a color they could live with, and the sheet wanted a row. So “document converter” became the north star, and eighty related titles grew under it like moss.

This chapter exists to stop that moss.

A word you can defend is a word that survives four questions in one sitting: **does anyone need this, can we actually compete, can we mention the product without a cough, and is the effort sane compared with the likely path?** If you cannot answer all four, the word is a diary prompt. It can still live on a later pile. It cannot claim this week’s only writing day.

---

## The Four-Question Gate

The Sea handbook lines the gate as demand, then competition, then monetization, then a rough ROI. I will keep that order, because reversing it is how teams write what is easy or what is glamorous.

**Demand.** Is this a question people already ask, in language you can find outside your own hopes? Volume estimates help and lie. A SERP with real ads, real forums, and real competing pages is better evidence than a single tool’s monthly number. So is a Book 1 complaint that already used the phrase. If nobody is asking, you are not “too early.” You are journaling in public.

**Competition.** Who occupies the results today, and with what kind of document? A difficulty score is a coarse ruler for “how much off-site trust might it take to budge what is already here.” It is not a prophecy. Low difficulty next to a page of official brand results is still a wall. High difficulty next to a pile of stale, thin posts might be a door. You have to look.

**Monetization.** Can this page mention Thursday Packet — or your product — without a false voice? The handbook’s 0–3 commercial-potential scale is a teaching tool I will steal because it is honest about capacity:

| Score | Meaning | How the page may speak | Priority |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 3 | You are a direct solution | Show the product and the reason to pay | First |
| 2 | You are a strong helper | Teach the hard way, name the limit, then the product | Next |
| 1 | Weakly related | A light pointer, or skip | Later |
| 0 | You cannot mention the product without a cough | Do not write it | Never, unless you have a media-site job this book is not primarily for |

A running-shoe sketch from the handbook makes the scale obvious: a query for the best marathon shoe is a 3 for a shoe maker; what to do about knee pain might be a 2; the history of the marathon is a 1; what music to run to is a 0. Translate that to Thursday Packet and “repair client names in a Thursday export” can be a 3. “What is a PDF?” is usually a 0. “How to keep typographic names from breaking in markup-to-export pipelines” can be a 2 or a 3, depending on how you write it. “History of office typography” is a 1 at best.

**ROI, said without a finance costume.** You have one serious writing week. A query that would take a report you cannot produce, or a link profile you cannot honestly earn this quarter, is a later-pile item even if the volume shines. A narrower query you can answer with a test you already ran may be the whole sprint.

Capacity is always smaller than the sheet. Sort by these four, not by volume.

## Difficulty Scores Are Gauges, Not Oracles

I will not baptize any vendor’s “KD” as science.

Different tools compute different numbers. Comparing them as if they shared a ruler is a party trick. The useful question underneath is plainer: **to displace what already sits in the first results, how much external recognition have those pages already collected?** That is a threshold gauge. It is not a promise that a low number means you will be first, or that a high number means you should not try a tighter variant.

Two rules travel well:

1. Prefer work in a band your current site can actually reach, unless you have a reason and a longer clock.  
2. Always walk the results. A monopoly of official brands, app stores, or documentation hubs can make a “easy” score dishonest.

If your process is “filter by KD, write, publish,” you have skipped the only part of the tool that was telling the truth: it made a list. The list still needs a human in the results.

### Formulas that may sort, and may not decide

The handbook also keeps a few sorting formulas with names that sound more official than they are. One family compares how many pages use the phrase in their title with how often the phrase is searched. Another weights that ratio with a difficulty score. A third tries, roughly, to set traffic potential against the effort of earning trust. I will not reprint them as if they were physics. I will say what they are for: **they reorder a candidate list so you look at likely long-tail work first.** The veto remains a manual SERP walk and a monetization judgment.

If a formula ever becomes the reason you publish, you have handed your week to an abbreviation.

## One Query, One URL (Until You Can Explain the Split)

Teams waste months letting two pages fight, or letting one page hoard six jobs.

Before you write, say which URL owns the query. If two drafts want the same job, merge them or send one to the later pile. If one draft wants “what is markup,” “how to repair names,” and “pricing,” split it, or admit you are writing a homepage and stop calling it an article.

Expansion is allowed. It is also how moss starts. Related questions belong in a cluster only when they share a job and can point, with honest internal links, to a hub or a product page. Brand terms — your name, your product, typo variants of your name — are a different species. They are memory, not a cold market. Bid on them in paid search when they leak to competitors, as Book 3 already allowed. Do not confuse them with a plan to win a category you have not earned.

Typo targeting of *other people’s* brands, especially in a way that impersonates, is not a growth hack in this book. It is a way to inherit legal and trust problems. If a typo is a genuine user error for a generic word, treat it as a redirect or a gentle note, not as a trap.

## Find Words from Sites, Sites from Words

You do not have to start with a tool.

**Site to words.** Walk a site that already serves the job. Which titles appear, which hubs exist, which questions show up in their headings and FAQs? This is reconnaissance, not a license to paraphrase their pages into your voice.

**Words to sites.** Take a candidate query and ask who already wins it. Are they docs, marketplaces, forums, thin blogs, or a product like yours? The container of the results tells you what to build. If the results are all videos, a text essay may be swimming upstream. If they are all forum threads, a clean tool page plus a worked example may be the increment. If they are all official docs, you may be a 0.

**Landing pages as witnesses.** Your own unpaid and paid landing pages already speak. Search Console queries, paid search terms from Book 3, and support mail are often better seeds than a “related keywords” dump.

Google’s public search operators — `site:` and a title-scoped operator (commonly written as in-title) — remain a low-cost radar when you use them as radar. Recheck current syntax. Do not treat a count of results as a market size.

Notice what the card refuses. It refuses to let the fattest volume claim the week. It refuses to split two complaint phrasings into two weak URLs. It refuses a definition page that cannot mention the product without a cough.

The builder, looking at this card, will feel poorer. That feeling is the point. Poverty of scope is how a small team earns a click.

## Where the Gate Falsifies Itself

**Media sites and ad-supported publications can justify some 0-score pages** if their business is the pageview and they have a separate, honest system for it. Book 3 already drew a perimeter around selling ads versus buying ads. If that is your job, say so. Do not smuggle those pages into a product sprint and call them “topical authority.”

**A genuinely new name for a job can have low measured demand and still be worth one careful page** if Book 1 already proved the job with complaints and a priced ask. You are then teaching a phrase, not chasing a volume. One page. Not eighty. Chapter 7 will call this a citable concept, not a link scheme.

**Local, seasonal, or tiny-language markets can make volume tools look like graves.** Walk the results anyway. Talk to the handful of people who already pay you. The gate still holds; the tool is just ruder.

**Internal search logs and support tags can outrun public volume.** Use them. They are complaints in a smaller room.

---

## FAQ (Choosing Without a Parade)

**Must I buy a keyword tool?** No. A tool can speed a list. Official search consoles, manual SERP walks, competitor headings, and your own complaint ledger can start a map. Buy a tool when the list is large enough that sorting is the bottleneck, not when you need courage.

**Can I write the 0-score piece if I already know it well?** You can journal it elsewhere. This sprint’s scarce day should not go to a page that cannot carry the product or the job.

**What if the SERP is mixed?** Name the dominant container and the increment you can add. Mixed results are a reason to be specific, not a reason to write a suitcase.

---

## Takeaway

**Rule:** A query earns a page only when demand, competition, monetization, and effort can share a sentence.

**This week:** Fill one keyword-to-page card for no more than seven candidate queries. Put the fat-head city on the later pile unless it truly is your job. Pick one owner URL for the sprint.
